## Glossary

In this book, I am using words which you might not be familiar with yet. For each one, here is a quick definition. Please refer to this index during your study of the book.

- Big Idea: A central, compelling concept or promise in a piece of persuasive communication that captures attention and drives interest by addressing a deep desire or need.

- Call to Action (CTA): A prompt designed to encourage a specific action, such as “Buy Now” or “Sign Up.”

- Copywriting: The art and science of writing text (or copy) for the purpose of persuasion, often used in marketing and sales to drive action, such as purchasing a product or signing up for a service.

- Cost of Acting: The tangible and intangible price associated with taking a specific action, such as effort, money, or time.


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 12 -->

- Cost of Not Acting: The implied consequences or missed opportunities resulting from inaction.

- Desire: A synonym for problem. Desire means wanting to go from situation A to situation B because situation B is seen as better. Being stuck in situation A or not being in situation B yet is a problem, an unwelcome fact.

- Direct Response: A type of marketing designed to elicit an immediate response from the audience, such as making a purchase or signing up for a service. It focuses on measurable results and clear calls to action.

- Ethos: The credibility or ethical appeal used to persuade an audience based on the speaker's character and authority.

- Exclusivity: The appeal of being part of a select group, enhancing desirability through scarcity or status.


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 13 -->

- Framing: Presenting information in a way that influences perception and decision-making.

- Hook: A compelling opening element in persuasive communication designed to grab attention immediately and draw the audience into the message.

- Immediate Desire: Logical and binary problems that trigger straightforward desires. These are often negative desires stemming from lack (e.g., fixing a broken car) but can occasionally be positive (e.g., spontaneous acts of kindness).

- Intrinsic Motivation: Motivation driven by internal rewards, such as personal satisfaction or fulfillment.

- Kairos: The opportune moment for persuasion, ensuring timing aligns with the audience’s receptiveness.

- Lede: The opening section of a piece of copy designed to grab attention and set the tone for the rest of the message. It is often used to


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 14 -->

highlight the most compelling aspect of the

offer or story.

- Lead: Potential customer or prospect who has shown interest in a product or service and is considered a likely candidate for conversion into paying clients.

- Logos: Logical appeal; persuading an audience through reason and evidence.

- Long-Term Desire: Profound aspirations shaping identity over time. These often involve significant transformations (e.g., achieving fitness goals) and can stem from both negative and positive desires.

- Loud Persuasion: A form of persuasion characterized by in-your-face claims, shady tactics, and plain lies aimed at selling at all costs. While it can be profitable initially, it usually works only once per customer because it erodes trust over time. It starts loud but eventually becomes ignored as people recognize its deceitful nature.


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 15 -->

- Mimetic Desire: The phenomenon where desires are shaped by observing what others want or value.

- Negative Desire: A force driven by lack and fear of additional lack. It works in cycles where the fulfillment of one desire leads to another lack.

- Neuro-Linguistic Programming (NLP): A psychological approach that explores the connection between neurological processes, language, and behavioral patterns learned through experience. It is often used in persuasion to model successful behaviors and improve communication effectiveness.

- North Star Values: Fundamental beliefs that guide decisions and represent an individual’s ultimate aspirations and identity.

- Pathos: An emotional appeal used in persuasion to evoke feelings in the audience.

- Positive Desire: Aspirations driven by creativity and self-expression rather than lack or fear.


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 16 -->

- Projection: A synonym for dream. When you dream of something, you picture it in your mind. It evokes great feelings and emotions but remains virtual. Projecting means thinking about something in the future, imagining yourself in this situation already.

- Quiet Persuasion: A subtle yet powerful form of persuasion aimed at achieving long-term results without resorting to unethical tactics. It focuses on ideas, mechanisms, stories, authenticity, and building trust over time rather than making bold claims. Quiet persuasion avoids manipulation while still delivering strong results by aligning with logical and emotional principles.

- Scarcity: The idea that people value things more highly when they perceive them as scarce or limited.

- Schemas: Deep-seated patterns of thought and behavior often formed early in life that


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 17 -->

influence decision-making and emotional

responses.

- Short-Term Desire: Temporary urges requiring fulfillment but not involving fundamental identity changes (e.g., hunger). These desires can also be positive (e.g., dancing spontaneously).

- Silent Persuasion: A timid form of persuasion characterized by shyness and self-doubt. This approach lacks confidence and fails to create impact because it remains unheard.

- Social Proof: A psychological phenomenon where people copy the actions of others in uncertain situations to determine correct behavior.

## Chapter I: What No-One Told Me When I Started

“The biggest secret about persuasion is not hidden in complex techniques. It is in your ability to update your own mental software.”

When I discovered persuasion, it was through the lens of copywriting. Copywriting is persuasion and sales in print. It teaches how to write to sell. And being an introvert, I felt instantly attracted to it.

At first, I could not wrap my head around what it was, or what it meant. The person who became my mentor had just sent me a boatload of 50-page documents called “sales letters,” and commanded I read them in full. Skimming through the endless pages, I remember asking myself: “How can anyone possibly read all that?”

But the results did not lie. I knew that many people, called “copywriters,” not only made a living writing these bizarre letters, but some of them were making more in a single month than everybody I

The first piece of advice I received was straightforward:

Forget everything you know about writing and

persuasion. Do not try to understand what is

going on through your old lenses; start from

zero, listen closely, be observant, identify

patterns, and learn to replicate them without

asking too many questions.

This is the absolute best piece of advice I have ever received, as it works wonders when you are around professionals. You just shut up, accept you know nothing, and let yourself be infused with radical, results-oriented, knowledge.

But it is also the worst piece of advice when you are not surrounded by professionals. Most people do not have the luxury of being trained at the country’s leading Direct Response publishing company. Most people start on their own, as freelancers, or small business entrepreneurs, and do not have access to refined, tried and tested processes. All they know is

Which, of course, flops. The luckiest people, those who have been successful through guesswork, are really the unluckiest, because they are unconsciously cementing these wrong ideas forever into their brain. ## Chapter II: Everything Stems from Desire

“Everything stems from desire. It is the basis of any form of persuasion. But when you look deeper, you realize it is also at the basis of life itself.”


### Desire as a Life Force

Desire is the basis of any form of persuasion. But when you look deeper, you realize it is also at the basis of life itself. Do you know the number one characteristic of living organisms? While inanimate objects like rocks indefinitely stay the same, living organisms (Monera, Protista, plants, fungi, and animals) all strive to expand. “To expand” means to grow bigger or more extensive. It also means moving forward in space, to cover more ground. So, you must understand that life equals movement, and that there is no life without movement. Just take your own body as an example: Your blood cells move to transport oxygen, your lungs move to breathe, your heart moves to pump blood, etc. Movement single-


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 40 -->

handedly keeps you alive. But what drives movement? What makes it possible? This life force we call “desire.”

When you think of desire, you think about wanting an object, a person, or a state. In English, desire is strongly linked to seduction and sex. This is not the case in Latin, where the word desiderare stems from de sidere— “from the stars,” and it means “to wish,” “to want,” “to like,” “to desire,” “to long”. And interestingly, desidero (“I desire”), also means “I miss,” “I lack,” “I need,” “I lose.” Desire has often been understood as lacking something and wanting that very thing. And it makes sense! Do you not strongly want things you do not have? I certainly do. But as we will see, this definition of desire as a force striving for completeness (filling in the gaps) has its limitations.


### Projection and Feedback Loop

Inside the brain, desire stems from the motivational system. Its main neurotransmitter is dopamine, largely considered as the “pleasure


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 41 -->

hormone.” Scientists used to think dopamine was the reward neurotransmitter, and that our brain only released dopamine after being rewarded for our action, making this action intrinsically pleasurable, and thus motivational.

Old model: I eat a burger → I feel pleasure and satisfaction → my brain releases dopamine to anchor this behavior.

That was so, until Ivan Pavlov showed through his now famous conditioning experiment that dopamine release happens before the reward, in anticipation. Pavlov trained dogs to associate the sound of a bell with receiving food. He anchored this behavior so much so that, after a while, simply ringing the bell caused the dogs to salivate.


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 42 -->

New model: I am about to order a burger → I anticipate the

pleasure and satisfaction → my brain releases dopamine to anchor this behavior.

It turns out the prospect of a virtual reward triggers a pleasurable dopamine release; not the reward itself. And I would like to accentuate the word virtual, here. Virtual means unrealized but expected. It means that you picture the burger in your mind, and even taste and smell it, without it existing. In Pavlov’s experiment, the dog was picturing the treat as if it were already eating it, hence the salivation.

But where does this treat, or burger, exist? If the reward is virtual, then it does not exist in the real world. Instead, it exists in our heads, in what I call a “projection,” a virtual world of imagination. And interestingly, this projection has a direct influence on the subject’s biological processes and behavior.


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 43 -->

Projections are virtual futures whose desirability is set by our motivational system. These projections can be positive and desirable, as well as negative and undesirable. If I were to present you with a button which would remove all your limbs when you press it, you would avoid it at all costs, because you would project the consequences into your existing reality. In this case you would actively act to prevent this future from ever happening, going as far as feeling stressed and uncomfortable whenever the button is close to being pressed, which is another form of response. In fact, you would probably do everything you can to ensure the button remains untouched, like sealing it into an uncrackable safe. In other words, you would act, because all life forms strive for expansion and self-preservation, while avoiding death, harm, and generally unpleasant experiences. So, we can assume that life acts to either achieve pleasure or avoid displeasure. This is what psychologists call “the pleasure principle.” Now, there are at least two


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 44 -->

exceptions: Delayed gratification, and paradoxical gratification.

Delayed gratification is when humans anticipate a great reward after a cycle of pain. If I offered to punch you in the face, and then give you a million dollars, you would probably accept that offer because the reward is more desirable than the projected pain required to get it. Discipline works the same way: Suffer now, reap the rewards later. This process can also be reversed to our disadvantage. This is called instant gratification, which gets us into a temporary and ever fleeting state of pleasure, creating a vicious circle of dopamine pumping leaving us numb, unfulfilled, and unhappy in the long run.

Paradoxical gratification is when people, through a skewed motivational system, expect pleasure or release from pain through directly harmful experiences. Mortification of the flesh, masochism, and suicide are examples of paradoxical gratification.


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 45 -->

In the end, whether the path to gratification is direct or indirect, conventional or deviant, desire always aims to reach a positive finality. Because desire moves life forward.


### Negative Desire vs. Positive Desire

Until now, I have been exclusively talking about negative desire. Negative desire is the force moving life toward sustenance by filling its void and avoiding new voids. And it acts from a position of lack, and fear of additional lack. Negative desire works in cycles, because an achieved state is always the beginning of a new lack cycle. I may not desire the same object, but I will desire something once again.

Negative desire:

Lack → Motivation → Result → Lack → […]

Negative desire is wanting what you do not have. And since life remains incomplete by nature, you never stop desiring. Now, this modality of desire


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 46 -->

is linked to an object, whether concrete or abstract. For instance, when you are hungry, you lack food. We can even assume that you lack a specific type of food, the one you crave or like. Imagine being hungry right now, and I present you with food you hate. Would you eat it? “Well, how hungry am I?” you would ask instead. Fulfilling a need or a desire only works if the projected reward is greater than the projected pain required to get it.

One of the clearest examples of negative desire is weight loss. In the weight-loss industry, marketers and copywriters accentuate the pain points of being overweight (lack of confidence, health issues, social stigma) and position their product as the solution to fill that void. Negative desire works through advertising what people lack, or are not: Here, overweight people are not slim and suffer from this difference.

But there is also another modality of desire: Positive desire. Positive desire does not act from a place of lack, frustration, or fear. It is not a need insomuch as it is a possibility.


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 47 -->

Positive desire is creation. It is egoless. It is not about self-gratification, self-fulfillment, or self-actualization. It is because it can be. It is because it must be.

When Vincent Van Gogh started his painting career at 27 years old, he did not know (and how could he?) that he only had 10 years to live. And during these 10 years, few things went right for him. He sold a dozen paintings, but not nearly enough to earn a living. He felt misunderstood, rejected and shunned. And his mental health suffered. In fact, if it had not been for his beloved brother and lifelong supporter, Theo, he would probably have ended his life much earlier than he did. Yet despite these challenges and contradictory signs, Van Gogh pursued his vocation. How could this happen? Why did Van Gogh not just give up on life? Well, vocation comes from the Latin vocare, “to call” or in this case, “to be called.” Van Gogh felt a calling to painting. He got attracted to it, almost against his will, and kept on working on improving his skill despite the many challenges. Nothing could stop him from painting.


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 48 -->

Not pain, not poverty, not even loneliness. In fact, these were the fuel to his creativity. In his letter #155 to his brother Theo, Vincent writes:

Someone has a great fire in his soul, and

nobody ever comes to warm themselves at it,

and passers-by see nothing but a little smoke at

the top of the chimney and then go on their way.

So now what are we to do, keep this fire alive

inside, have salt in ourselves, wait patiently [?]

What is that “great fire”? It is the necessity of being who we are destined to be; a silent yet irresistible force attracting us toward greater meaning. It matters little whether others can see it, or even appreciate it. What matters is keeping this flame alive, against all and everything. Art is that fire, it is a way to not only express who we are but become who we are. Positive desire does not seek a finality. It is the finality, a greater ascension into being, which explains why most artists persist in their art despite hardships and failures. There are other types of vocations: Religious, spiritual, and even


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 49 -->

entrepreneurial. Whatever form positive desire takes, you must understand two things:

1. Whereas negative desire is about chasing

something… Positive desire is about being

attracted or pulled toward something, despite

not having a finality.

2. Positive desire is not about fulfilling a need

or a lack, but about the necessity of

expressing oneself through new and evolving

forms despite adversity. It is often not a

choice, but a way of being.

Some marketing campaigns align themselves with positive desire. Apple’s “Think different,” rather than focusing on the product or addressing a specific lack, appealed to people’s creativity and uniqueness. The campaign did not sell computers; it sold the vision of being a world-changing thinker. Now, does positive desire make for effective marketing? I do not think it does, at least not in the scope of a small business, because without a specific object of lack, positive desire is intrinsically more


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 50 -->

vague than negative desire. This leads me to make an important distinction between three other types of desires.


### The Time Spectrum of Desire

Desire comes in two flavors:

1. Chasing what we lack (negative desire).

2. Being pulled forward by a superior

expression of who we are (positive desire).

But this is not sufficient to understand desire, as there is another dimension to consider as well: Time. Desire expresses itself differently depending on its immediateness or lengthiness. There are 3 different types of positive and negative desires.


#### 1. Immediate Desires

These are logical and binary problems that trigger straightforward desires. The need is clear, and the solution is obvious. There is no long-term transformation involved. Most immediate desires stem from a lack, making them inherently negative.


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 51 -->

For example, if you lock yourself out of your house, your problem is binary—you are either locked outside or you are not. Your desire here is simple: To access your home again. This desire does not involve any change in identity or transformation. Although more uncommon, immediate positive desires do exist. Gestures of spontaneous kindness or helping someone without expecting anything in return can be seen as immediate positive desires, provided they are not driven by a need for recognition. Keep in mind that immediate desires arise from logical problems, where the answer is the exact opposite of the problem:

- Your car breaking down ↔ fixing your car.

- Losing your keys ↔ finding your keys.

- Failing an exam ↔ passing the exam.

People who fix immediate problems (plumbers, engineers, mechanics, etc.) never run out of business. It is one of the most, if not the most, reliable business models. Depending on the complexity of that


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 52 -->

immediate problem, it can be extremely lucrative as well.


#### 2. Short-Term Desires

Short-term desires are those occasional urges and temporary states-of-being that require fulfillment but do not involve a fundamental change in identity. Unlike immediate desires, which are binary and resolved quickly, short-term desires are gradual and cyclical. They can be easier, or more difficult to solve, but their temporary nature means they can swing back and forth like a pendulum. Most short-term desires are negative, as they often arise from lack. For example, hunger is a short-term negative desire because:

- It stems from a lack of nourishment.

- It is not binary; you can feel peckish or

famished.

- It affects your mood and behavior.

- When fulfilled, hunger will return after some

time.


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 53 -->

Other types of short-term desires include benign physical problems, as well as most emotions and feelings, such as sadness, melancholy, tiredness… Short-term positive desires also exist. These include sudden urges for expression, such as dancing when you hear your favorite song on the radio, petting a cat on the street, or spending time with a friend. These desires are driven by the joy of the moment rather than a need to fill a void.


#### 3. Long-Term Desires

Long-term desires are profound aspirations that shape our identity over extended periods. Unlike short-term desires, which are temporary and recurring, long-term desires represent significant transformations that, once achieved, often create a lasting change in who we are. The fulfillment of long-term desires is usually the culmination of years of persistent effort, strong will, and discipline. While the trigger event that officially marks the achievement may be immediate, the journey to reach


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 54 -->

that point is gradual and often arduous. Consider, “getting in shape”:

- It requires weeks, months, or even years of

consistent work.

- It demands repeated effort, commitment, and

self-discipline. Delayed gratification.

- Achieving your desired level of fitness alters

how others perceive you and how you see

yourself.

- You become part of a new category of people

who share a similar identity. Fitness buffs,

biohackers, bodybuilders, etc.

The value of long-term desires lies in the significance of the outcome, which often matches the difficulty of the endeavor. As Eddie Cantor famously said, “It takes 20 years to make an overnight success.” While the defining moment of achievement may appear sudden, the groundwork necessary to make it happen usually takes considerably more time and effort. Some long-term desires can be fulfilled more quickly than others, but the general principle


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 55 -->

remains: The more desirable the outcome, the more challenging the journey to attain it. This is why the realization of long-term desires often leads to a profound sense of accomplishment and personal growth. Long-term desires can stem from both negative and positive desires, but I believe positive ones work better in the end, as they come from a place of security rather than lack. Security and faith strengthen abnegation and discipline, increasing the chances of success. Lack, although biologically imperative, is a suppressive force rooted in comfort; it rarely permits extraordinary feats of prowess. I can become slim if I want the confidence and positive self-image it would provide me, but I need to reach out for an even stronger will if I want to be slim and stay slim all my life.

Long-term desires do not stem from the void. They are often the result of what I call “North star values,” which are the most important values an individual carries all their life. If we believe desire is a physical force which allows life to move, then North star values give this force a precise direction.


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 56 -->


### North Star Values

I remember the first year of my copywriting career. I was talking with another junior copywriter who had joined the firm three months earlier. We were both sharing our projections: How we saw the future, what we expected from our careers at the firm, how we defined a successful life. I remember clearly saying that I did not see myself working there for more than a few years, because I did not enjoy office work. Instead, I much preferred following my own schedule, having the freedom to choose what I wanted to do when I wanted to do it. I can remember talking about this and having all sorts of projections inside my head. I could imagine myself working from home on my personal projects, being around my children as they grow up, working late in the night and waking up late in the morning. This, to me, was “it:” My personal North star.

Standing beside me, my friend nodded but quickly disagreed. He could not bear such a disorganized life. “I need routines and precise schedules,” he answered, “or else I’d be losing my


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 57 -->

mind.” His personal North star was mainly about being considered and recognized by his peers, earning a good living, being anchored in a stable and secure situation. All the things which would have been impossible had he been a freelancer. North star values are the opposite of “antivalues”: Things we do not like, things which are not us. We like certain things because we hate other things.

Long-term desires follow these North star values in their sights. They are the ultimate projection, where desire leads us. Immediate and short-term desires, when encapsulated inside bigger, long-term desires, also follow these North star values. Recognizing these values is paramount when you want to persuade someone, because they are the literal definition of who they are. No-one ever goes against their North star values, because it defines them. Going against their values would be suicide or self-destruction. The secret is that once you identify someone’s North star values and you align yourself with them, you can take full control of their behavior, providing you make your intentions clear and pure.


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 58 -->

Copywriters (me included) have also called North star values “deep benefits,” because they are the ones you end up with when you ask your customers “why” they would want something. Why, for instance, it is important to them to buy this life insurance:

1. “Because it makes me feel at ease, knowing

that my family will be cared for if I die.”

… To which you would ask another “why”:

2. “Because I want to ensure my family’s

financial security and well-being even after I

am gone.”

… To which you would ask another “why”:

3. “Because I feel responsible for providing for

my loved ones and want to leave a lasting

positive impact on their lives.”

And there you have it; the third “why” is the North star value. Here, it is about being seen and remembered as a positive force in others’ lives.


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 59 -->

North star values are often interpersonal: They relate to others. They are often about status, meaning how people see you, and how you see yourself. How, in other words, you position yourself in the hierarchy of human beings. In Friedrich Nietzsche’s words, are you a slave, or a master? Are you selfish, of low value, and an overall negative force? Or are you valuable, powerful, and reliable force? North star values are the answer. Some people place personal gain and personal power over everything, even if it leads to cowardice, treason, and unfaithfulness. They are by definition “anti-social.” Others know that helping others is a great way to help oneself and achieve personal fulfillment. They are by definition “social.” Everything we do, we always do in relationship to others, and ourselves. Everything is status. Everything is social.

Anyone can cultivate strong North star values, but it does take a certain amount of courage to build your own. Most people acquire North star values through other people: Their parents, their friends, their favorite celebrities, or fictional characters.


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 60 -->


### Mimetic Desire

Desire is often mimetic, meaning that we acquire new desires by watching others. Take love triangles, for instance. Jack loves Sally. He tells his good friend Paul about his feelings. Paul watches Jack chase Sally and begins feeling something for her. He flirts with her, hoping to get her for himself. This is a typical case of mimetic desire. Would Paul have flirted with Sally if he did not know Jack was in love with her? Perhaps not, as Paul would not have even noticed Sally if Jack had not told him about her.

Jack made a mistake by telling Paul about Sally. Why? Because he redirected his energy and focus on her. Desire is an energy which requires movement, evolution, and change. If Paul has a bit too much free romantic energy, maybe because he has been celibate for a while, it will get channeled at the first opportunity, just like a magnet attracts the first metal in proximity. Knowing that Jack fancies Sally only makes her appear more desirable, because it adds a social element to the mix. “If good men like Jack fancy Sally,” Paul thinks unconsciously, “then Sally


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 61 -->

must be a desirable woman. Thus, I must have her to myself, in order to show Jack that I am equal, and even better, than he is.” But here is the caveat. Paul would not have fancied Sally if he did not value Jack. If Jack had been a bum on the street, Paul would not have considered Jack’s interest as valuable. It is precisely because Jack is a valued friend that Paul fancies Sally. In other words, Paul models his interests onto Jack’s, because admiration is a form of envy.1

Think about the sheer number of people who live their lives according to other people’s needs and desires, and how many people buy products to resemble celebrities and influencers. This is a way for them to reframe their own self-image, in order to appear to others and themselves as more valuable. Does mimetic desire actually make them more valuable as human beings? You would be right to doubt it.

1 You can feel this mimetic desire strongly in Proust’s Swann’s Way, when Marcel (the narrator) models his desire onto Swann, whom he sees as a quasi-deity. Marcel falls in love with Gilberte, Swann’s own daughter, to get close to Swann, while challenging his paternal power.


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 62 -->

Mimetic desires rarely apply to immediate and short-term desires. You do not usually mimic your need for food or sleep onto others. In the same way, you would not model an immediate desire, such as getting your phone repaired because you dropped it on the floor, on the desires of others. Mimetic desires apply to mostly long-term, social desires and North star values. Remember: long-term desires are transformations getting you (hopefully) closer to your North star values. They are not the same thing. Most people develop fewer than a dozen long-term desires throughout their lives, including finding love, being fit, having a successful career, or traveling the world. They go after them to get closer to their North star values, their ideal projection.

If some of the distinctions I made above are not perfectly clear, this is because life is a complex entanglement of immediate, short-term, long-term negative, positive, and sometimes mimetic desires, as well as North star values. Not all of our desires fit neatly into one single box. To clearly understand a situation, you would need to lay down every single


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 63 -->

element affecting it. This is possible for basic examples, but it is much more complicated in real life. Do not worry about understanding every situation extensively. Develop a general understanding of the elements I have shown you, and you will soon be able to get human behavior on an intuitive level.

Still—there is one last idea we must cover, and it is the cornerstone of this theory of desire.


### The Depth of Projection

The clearest way to think about desire is to translate it into concrete, mechanistic elements. So do the following: Imagine desire as a factory. What is a factory? Generally speaking, it is a place of transformation, where raw materials are turned into refined objects. You start with sand, and you end up with glass pitchers. You start with iron ore, and you end up with forks. This transformation turns chaotic elements into refined and useful objects. In other words, it turns random noise into a clear signal. A signal is information we can understand, interpret,


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 64 -->

and differentiate. When you turn on the dial of your radio, you hear indistinguishable noise that sounds the same, no matter the frequency. It is only once you reach a station that you can tell it apart from the noise and all the other stations. When you listen to Dusk 106.5, you can be sure that you are not listening to noise, nor any other station. This is the definition of specificity: Referring to specific, accountable, and distinguishable objects. Or, said differently, when you can link a word to a singular reality.

Desire works the same way. Everything starts with raw feelings, blurred sensations and inexplicable attraction: Noise. When you see a product which “tickles your fancy” (note the use of an immediate physiological reaction here) and without being able to explain why, you know you want it. It does not require an explanation. It is immediately evident. It is the same with love and lust: You see someone, you find them beautiful, and voilà… You are hooked. This is because desire begins in the unconscious mind (the “gut”) and


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 65 -->

climbs up to the conscious rational mind once you recognize what you feel.

Only then do you start to understand the reasons behind your desire. The projection, which started deep in the chaotic unconscious, takes on a clearer shape. And the more you think about your object of desire, the more vivid this projection becomes. Maybe you have dreamt of running a successful business before. At first, you felt a blurry attraction toward money and success. How much money? You do not know. What does success even mean? No idea! In this first stage, desire is abstract and formless. It is purely sensual and does not express itself in words, because it hides deep inside the unconscious. In other words, you have not rationalized it. You just want… something! The longer this desire lasts, the more time you have to refine it, separate it from what you do not want, give it words, shapes, colors, expressions. In other words, you build a clearer and clearer positive projection, by separating it from all the negative projections you do not want.


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 66 -->

Projection is at the center of desire, and this is what most marketers and copywriters miss. Most of them think desire is about longing for single objects. A few moments ago, I even explained to you that being hungry meant lacking food and lacking a specific type of food we prefer. Well, I was not entirely correct. In reality (and to stick with this example), being hungry is the lack, not of individual types of food, but of satiation, and the pleasure leading to it. It is about the projection of a world where you eat food you love, experience intense pleasure, and feel a deep sense of satisfaction afterwards. It is also about getting away from the projection of lack, or in this case, starvation. Just like being stuck between two magnets.

You are hungry because you are not satiated. Satiation just happens to be possible through different types of food, some of them you prefer over others. Let us say you like burgers, well burgers are not the end of the road (the finality); rather it is your preferred means of achieving the feeling of satiation, while escaping from the feeling of starvation. So,


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 67 -->

when you think about projection, you must take into account:

1. The finality: satiation, which is the end

feeling.

2. The means through which it comes: the

burger.

3. But also, the logical consequences of this

finality.

Someone who is starving acts differently from someone who is satisfied. Starvation induces physiological, mental, as well as social consequences. Their stomach could hurt (physiological). They may be irritable (mental). They could even be ready to steal food (social). This would not happen if that person were satiated. I would even go as far to say that the single act of fulfilling one’s needs can create a whole new reality. In other words, you really are one single decision away from a whole new life, whether infinitely worse or infinitely better. Copywriters and marketers must know that projection goes further than simply having


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 68 -->

one’s desire fulfilled. When you sell a car, you do not just sell the car. Instead, you are selling the projection of a brand-new world, where the car is simply a means to an end, which itself has almost infinite consequences. The sale is simply the promise that they will get their projection realized.

Old model:

A salesman showcases a car to his customer: He tells him about the car, its features, its mileage, its options.

Here the salesman focuses on what he erroneously perceives as the finality of the customer’s desire. But the customer shows little interest. Why? Because features, mileage, and options, are external objects, numbers, and figures. They do not involve him. By that, I mean that the mileage of the car will always remain like it is whether the customer buys the car or not. It is a separate reality, with little influence over the


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 69 -->

customer’s life. This is why, in this situation, persuasion is not effective.

New model: Another salesman showcases a car to his customer: he tells him about how he will feel while driving it, how others will see him, and how he will see himself when driving it. He tells him about how he will escape the current problems he is facing, which could get worse in the future. The car, its features, mileage, and options, are simply vessels allowing this new reality to take place.

In this example, what “works” is the fact that the car has a direct influence on the driver’s life. In fact, it represents an entry gate to a completely new world made of numerous desirable consequences. The customer does not desire the vehicle itself, but the entire network of associations, experiences, and social contexts it unlocks, including getting away from his current situation.


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 70 -->

“Picture yourself driving your favorite car along a winding coastal road, at sunset. The engine growls beneath you, responding to your touch. As you cruise, you notice admiring glances from fellow drivers and pedestrians, who approve of your taste. You feel a sense of pride and accomplishment wash over you, it is a symbol of how far you have come. It is your way of saying thank you to those who believed in you, and the perfect response to those who doubted you…”

Can you feel how this is more engaging and desirable than a simple catalog of the car’s features? Well, that is because, in this example, a salesperson would help you build a brand-new mental world by highlighting exactly what it would look like for you. They would not leave any stone unturned. In fact, at the end of their spiel, the world they would have built for you would be so real, you would already feel inside that car.

What they have done is simply rewire your desire machine, by giving it form. They have helped


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 71 -->

you construct a well-defined projection, which already feedback loops into reality.

Read this extract from Marcel Proust’s Swann’s Way which illustrates this point perfectly:

All day long, during these walks, I had been

able to muse upon the pleasure that there would

be in the friendship of the Duchesse

de Guermantes, in fishing for trout, in drifting

by myself in a boat on the Vivonne; and, greedy

for happiness, I asked nothing more from life,

in such moments, than that it should consist

always of a series of joyous afternoons. Here the narrator projects in the Duchesse de Guermantes’ friendship a whole new world of happiness and “joyous afternoons.” He does not want her friendship by itself, but as a way to live in a world of tranquil joy. But here is the catch: To the narrator, this world of tranquil joy would not be possible without the intermediary of his friendship with the Duchesse. For him it is the necessary mechanism making this world possible (when in reality, you do not need to befriend a Duchesse to enjoy sunny


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 72 -->

afternoons). This skewed perception is not rational or logical. It is emotional. The narrator fabricates an incorrect reason to befriend the Duchesse, which only comes as a way to satisfy an emotional desire. This is what the late great salesman Zig Ziglar (among others) means when he says that people buy for emotional reasons and justify their decisions with logic. People buy for emotional ends and make it possible through (often biased) logical means. At the end they get what they want, while avoiding feeling bad about it, because it seems logical, even though it is not.

People do not buy Bentleys, Porsches, or BMWs for their engineering. Not even for their line. Some people pretend they buy a car because of its aesthetics, but that is just their logical justification. Imagine if people started telling the truth about the real reasons behind their purchases, it would probably turn any casual conversation into too deep of a confession, and this is why everyone sticks to logical reasons, which makes them look more refined, serious, and intelligent.


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 73 -->

Of course, you can desire an object for its aesthetic value and properties. You would be correct to want to buy a pair of shoes because they are beautiful and well made. Not every purchase hides a deep psychoanalytical secret such as daddy or mommy issues, as Freud would postulate. With that said, most, if not all, purchases are about creating a new world. The object simply acts as the mechanism, the key to unlock it.

If all you wanted was logical, aesthetic, contentment, then you would not have to buy a car. You could simply buy a picture of that car, or a miniature, or just spend more time admiring that car when you saw it on the street or at the dealership. Your need for aesthetic contentment would be satisfied. In reality, you do not spend upwards of $200,000 on a car you could admire for free. There is a better reason: This car unlocks a whole new reality for you, the projection of a dream life. You are not only able to admire and drive this car every day, but you are also seen admiring and driving this car. It is not only an internal satisfaction, but a way

### What Is Persuasion?

As you have understood in the previous chapters, desire is the flow that moves people toward new worlds I call projections. And this is the basis of persuasion: A transfer of desire. - In example A, they see themselves without

### The Persuasion Process

These 8 main elements are the building blocks of the persuasion machine. The process is simple: Its goal is to convert desire into action:

DAPA: [Desire] → [Attention] → [Projection] → [Action]

If you know a little bit about marketing, you can already see the similarity with Gary Halbert’s AIDA framework. AIDA stands for: Attention, Interest,


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 118 -->

Desire, Action; it outlines the process a piece of sales Copy should follow to get the sale. While AIDA gives you a way to write sales copy, DAPA gives you a more general understanding of persuasion; namely that persuasion is desire turned into action. The idea is to strengthen the relationship, build an irresistible projection, showcase an easy solution, diminish the pain of the action, reduce the cost of acting and increase the perceived cost of not acting. But the million-dollar question is… How do you do it? Well, just like any system, persuasion is made of fixed elements and dynamic ones. The dynamic elements allow you to increase the chances of successful persuasion.

A strong message with a weak relationship creates resistance. A powerful projection without a clear path to action breeds frustration. Every part must work in perfect harmony.

### How to Be the Most Persuasive “You” Possible

says your wife. “Stop smoking,” says your oncologist.

#### Build Authority, Credibility, and Social Status

By now you may be familiar with the Milgram experiment. If you are not, here is a quick recap. In the early 1960s at Yale University, psychologist Stanley Milgram conducted an experiment where he instructed participants to administer what they believed were electric shocks to another person (actually an actor) whenever that person made a mistake in a fake memory test. Despite hearing screams of pain and pleas to stop, 65% of participants continued to deliver shocks up to the maximum 450-volt level—a deadly amount—simply because a researcher in a lab coat told them to continue. The experiment’s participants were ordinary people who, despite questioning the experiment at some point, chose to follow orders rather than challenge authority.

This experiment shows how persuasive authority figures can be, for several reasons. When doctors tell patients that they suffer from a deadly disease and that they must take a certain medication


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 125 -->

to survive, no matter how anti-Big Pharma they are, they usually comply. Because there is too much at stake (the “cost” of not acting), and because whether you consciously love or despise doctors, they have been given a symbolic title which oozes certainty. Doctors must “know” better, because this is what doctors do. This is how powerful titles are. They are synonymous with specific qualities, and the reason why most people complied during the Milgram experiment: if a scientist tells me what to do, that is because he knows what will happen next. He knows things I ignore. And if I do not listen, I cannot be sure of the result of my actions. Here, there is a perceived greater risk in listening to oneself, and a perceived lesser risk of listening to the authority.

The second reason behind this result is that there is a power-play behind each persuasion attempt. In fact, persuasion is at its core a channeling of desire; it is directed movement and energy which responds to push and pull forces. Imagine a primitive setting where a dad tells his son to gather wood outside for the fire. By asking this, he is effectively


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 126 -->

redirecting his son’s energy toward feeding their source of heat, thereby improving their chances of survival. If the son has other plans, such as playing by the river, there will be unwillingness and resistance from his side. The dad will then have to convince his son, raise his voice, threaten him with punishment, or even physically force him to do what he says. The child knows it. He knows that by denying his father’s request, he is escalating the situation, and not only risking an altercation which could lead to violence (and with him being the lesser man of the two because getting hurt would be inevitable) but even suffering a great loss of status in the family, such as being outcast and harming his own chances of survival. While the son can just comply out of love, he can also comply out of fear of loss in health, status, and survival chances.

In most real-life situations, though, all of it is happening at the same time, unconsciously. We make choices in a split second, without always thinking further about the consequences. This is why no matter what you sell, and no matter who you try


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 127 -->

to persuade, you have to play on these unconscious triggers such as authority, credibility and social status. The question you must ask yourself is: How can you show the persuadee you know more than them? Can you show credentials, awards, distinctions, titles, TV appearances, a network of powerful people, proof of your own success? If you cannot show any type of official authority, then you can show moral authority. You do this by focusing on what you have done in the past to help people like them. For instance, you can tell your story, highlighting the efforts you have made in the past to help others achieve their goals. Because there is no better proof than your own past results.


#### Show Them Your Past Results

Past results are the single most effective driver of credibility for one reason: They show reproducibility. Reproducible results build confidence by logically proving that you know the way from point A (the problem state) to point B (the dream state). Where most persuasionists go wrong is


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 128 -->

in thinking that the more testimonials they show, the more effective their persuasion attempt will be. This mental shortcut is based on the false assumption that people care enough to read these testimonials, and that even if they do not, they will at least be impressed by the names associated with them or by their sheer number. And while having the President of the USA say that you are a great guy sure is impressive… it may only improve your sales indirectly (if at all), by establishing your social status. Effective testimonials—meaning testimonials that directly drive sales—are neither about big names nor about how many you have. They are all about the proximity of experience between the testimonial and the reader.

Imagine that you are in a complex financial situation requiring a financial advisor capable of getting you out of potential trouble with the IRS. To solve your problem, you interview two financial professionals. The first one shows you a picture of himself with Keith Richards of the Rolling Stones, while the second one tells you about Frank, a regular


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 129 -->

guy who faced the same problem as you. While one tells you about the time Keith invited him on stage to smash a guitar, the other shows you how he helped Frank escape the IRS through a perfectly legal loophole. While having the first candidate as a friend might be exciting, we both agree that it is the second one who should become our tax advisor. Of course, I am exaggerating, but you see my point: Testimonials work not by impressing the reader but by signaling that there is a clear path from their personal situation to the outcome they desire. It is not about impressing them with big names or hundreds of testimonials but about proving that their exact problem can, in fact, be solved.

Knowing this, there’s no need to chase validation or social status, as these are passive attributes. Instead, focus on selecting the right case study, testimonial, or past result to show your potential customer. Because adapting your strategy to fit the person in front of you will always work better than a generic sales pitch trying too hard to impress its audience.

#### Be Confident and Charismatic

You and I both know how confidence and charisma increase your chances of persuasion success. But do not skip this section, as I will tell you what these qualities are really, how they work, and how to actually become more confident and charismatic on command. It may not be what you think.

Confidence is an interesting word, because as I told you in the previous section, it stems from reproducibility. Being confident has nothing to do with “being yourself,” or any other type of elusive qualities. Being confident means one thing only: Having the true knowledge that things will work as they should. Its opposite is doubt. People lack confidence because they do not understand how things work. True expertise is dead, and it has been replaced by fake HR values, which care more about virtue signaling and keeping appearances than finding the truth. Here is the thing: When you know the process, you never risk your neck. Things go as they should, and as they always have. Imagine


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 131 -->

having found the cure for cancer and being certain that it works for everybody. Would you hesitate a second before pitching it to investors? Confidence is knowing that it has nothing to do with you, and that a superior product that works is all you need. Confidence is only possible when you have nothing to lose, because you know you cannot lose. Doubts only appear when failure is a possibility. As a persuasionist, this should never happen to you. A few years ago, real-estate genius Craig Proctor came up with one of the most successful Unique Selling Propositions (USP) ever created: “Your home sold in 180 days, or I buy it myself.” This USP brought Proctor north of a billion dollars. Why does it work? Because Proctor found that the desire behind selling your home is immediate: You either have your home sold or not, there is no in-between. Now all he had to do was to find a way to create a 100%-win situation. So, if he could not sell their home, he would buy it himself. Now this is quite a confident offer, right? Overconfident even, some would say. Well, here’s the trick, by having this valuable USP, Proctor


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 132 -->

received hundreds of demands from interested leads. So many, in fact, that during his 20-year career, he was able to choose which home he wanted to sell— and refuse the ones he knew he could not. In the end, he had to buy only 2 homes in total, out of the thousands he successfully sold. A winning gambit. Confidence appears second, allowed by a reproducible and reliable offer. Salesmen with great products never run out of successes.

In the same fashion, and once again contrary to popular belief, charisma is not something you build consciously. Charisma is not the result of self-work, or techniques you read in books. In fact, building charisma consciously only leads to gauche techniques which never seem natural. In reality, someone is not charismatic by themselves, but always in relation to others. You are not charismatic. People find you charismatic. And this difference is crucial. According to René Girard, charisma is not the result of your own uniqueness, but of a mimetic desire. People see in you that which they would like to see in themselves. You become a conduit for


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 133 -->

collective fantasies—how the most successful and desirable people act. This is the difference between a magnetic and charismatic entrepreneur and a complete weirdo. Both are unique, both are “themselves.” But only one aligns with the zeitgeist’s desires, meaning what is seen as desirable today.

With that said, how do you increase your chances of being seen as charismatic? There is only one way: Absolute emotional distance. Remaining focused on the process like a tiger on its prey, while being as disinterested as possible from what surrounds it. When you sell for a living, focus all your energy on the product, its results for the audience, and their reproducibility. Stop trying to control everything else if you do not have this nailed down to a tee beforehand. Instead, show immovable trust. Like dominoes, everything else will fall into place.

Now, do not get me wrong, I am not saying that marketing does not matter, nor that you should stop caring about how well you dress, how nice you smell,


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 134 -->

or how legible your sales letters are. The wrapping matters as much as the object, but it cannot replace it—and does not matter if the object inside is broken. As with any process, elements have different importance and different orders of appearance. There is no good marketing without a great product. That’s the first step. And there is no great product without good marketing, that’s the second step.


#### Cultivate a “Total” Ethos

Ethos is one of the three pillars of classical Greek rhetoric, along with logos (logic) and pathos (emotion). For Aristotle, you can persuade people using logical arguments (such as syllogisms), emotional appeals, as well as showing the ethical contents of your character. This is ethos, which circles back to authority, credibility, and trustworthiness. Yet there is one element of ethos few ever mention: It refers to the implied relationship between you and your audience, and different relationships have different strengths and weaknesses. For each ethos you study, you will see


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 135 -->

shortcomings it must overcome to be more effective in persuasion. See, not all authorities are created equal. A doctor, a teacher, an elder brother, a priest, a king, all have different forms of authority. According to German sociologist Max Weber’s hierarchy of authority, we can even classify them under three categories of authority: traditional, charismatic, and legal:

- Traditional authority is rooted in heritage or

cultural norms: Elder brothers, priests, kings,

fathers…

- Charismatic authority works on the leader’s

personal appeal and vision: Experts, writers,

“gurus,” politicians…

- Legal authority is tied to formalized rules and

institutional roles: Doctors, policemen,

financial advisors…

Every highlighted role above answers to a category and has one or more shortcomings to overcome.


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 136 -->

A doctor receives his authority from science (objective knowledge), experience and his medical degree, which are all quite devoid of empathy and human connection. Thus, at one point in the persuasion process, there will be a lack of mutual understanding, even reluctance and suspicion from the patient, as they may not feel valued as human beings. Who has never felt misunderstood, ignored, or even disregarded by their doctor, like your life does not matter? This is the shortcoming of this ethos and the way to effectively improve it is simple. You can show clear empathy and understanding. Instead of seeing patients as scientific experiments, doctors must treat them with the utmost care and attention. And this simple shift in ethos can be a game changer. Of course, behind this exaggeration lies some truth, but an astute persuasionist will see how this Doctor is always on the audience’s side, no matter at whom he throws rocks, or even the size of the rocks. More on this later.

On the contrary, the “elder brother” (“I have been in your situation before”) ethos is full of good will and kindness but lacks definite credibility. Its advice can sound like wishful thinking and can even feel manipulative. This is why you must use excessive proof to make its arguments resonate on more than just an emotional level. You must show people (who become de facto your younger siblings) that you were not lucky, and that you found a reproducible method which works no matter their current situation and personal challenges.

Remember that whichever ethos suits you best, you must make it complete by focusing on what it

lacks. The key lies in what others with the same ethos forget to do. When they zig, you zag.


#### Get Their Permission to Lead

Authority commands obedience through status and power, which both affect the amount of compliance and resistance in the persuadee.

*The spectrum of authority:*

| | Low resistance | High resistance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| High compliance | Leadership | Tyranny |
| Low compliance | Imposture | Usurpation |

Pure unadulterated authority feels like forceful tyranny, and while it can garner compliance, people instinctively resist it. Imagine asking someone to


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 139 -->

raise their hand, then pushing down on it—they will push back. Resistance to force is universal. Conversely, credible authority—also known as leadership—faces high compliance and low resistance. This is the exact form of authority you want to manifest to be successful in persuasion.

To do this, you must first understand that no form of effective authority thrives in opposition. Tyrants are always met with rebellion because their authority is only in appearance. Free the oppressed from their chains, and they will kill their king. True leaders, however, command willing obedience even when they have no way to enforce it. And how do they achieve this? With similarity.

Similarity means that whatever status you have compared to your audience, you should base your arguments on what unifies you rather than separates you. Take Donald Trump as an example. On paper, he is the opposite of many Americans in the Bible Belt: a billionaire from New York versus individuals struggling in some of the poorest states in the country. Yet Trump has enjoyed immense popularity


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 140 -->

with this demographic. Why? Because they perceive him as similar in how he thinks, speaks, and values the world. They share common North Star values that transcend their surface differences. Donald Trump knows that you cannot talk down to people, no matter the size of your bank account. He will step down, only so he can talk to them face-to-face.

This principle also explains why it is so difficult to persuade someone with opposing political views. Conversations framed through opposition—like “left” versus “right”—create insurmountable divides because neither side wants to concede nor “lose.” How do you actually talk, and listen, then? By opening or reducing the frame so that it includes both sides. See, there are similarities between debaters. They could be part of the same town. The same state. The same country. The same human race. It does not matter; you will find common ground somewhere. Once you go beyond the opposition and into shared territories, you open the gates of discussion. Two Americans can have a constructive discussion about


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 141 -->

the state of their country; a Democrat and a Republican cannot.

Acting through a shared identity or common ground gets you permission to lead, because you show clear intentions, similar values, and proof you will keep your word.


### How to Better Understand “Them” Find Their Right Timing

Previously, during the Ethos section, I listed Aristotle’s pillars of persuasion being logos (logic), ethos (character) and pathos (emotion). But there is a fourth and lesser-known pillar called kairos. Kairos represents the right timing, the opportune moment for persuasion. Kairos refers to the persuadee’s personal context:

- Is it appropriate to persuade this audience?

- Is this the right time to do so?

- Will this audience be receptive to my

attempts?


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 142 -->

It would be easy to think that timing does not really matter, or that a working message will always work. This is unfortunately incorrect. Timing is one, if not the most important, factor in persuasion. A few years ago, I had the privilege of witnessing a rare sight. I saw one of the most successful launches of a financial program in my company’s history. No less than $1,000,000 in a single day. Was it the message? Although good, I would not think so. Was it marketing? No, this had been launched the same way as a dozen other less successful programs. This time, it was not different. It was the market. That month, the stock market had been surging. Spirits were high, and both amateur and qualified investors were actively seeking new investment opportunities. In other words, the timing was perfect. I believe that you should not launch a product if you think the timing is not right, or if you can sense that the market is not in the mood for it. Do not just rely on your gut feeling: Test the water. Talk about the idea, the opportunity, the projection, to clients, friends, and family. Do not tell them about the project you are


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 143 -->

launching, as this will skew their response positively. Instead, emit doubts about it. For instance, if you are preparing a product on real estate investment, try telling a client you are “Not sure it is the right time to invest in real estate.” If they agree or if they opt for a neutral standpoint, then you have the beginning of an answer. If they fiercely disagree with you, or tell you plans of doing the exact thing, then this means you might be onto something. For good measure, be sure to ask several people. The ideal would be, of course, to beta-test your idea on the market. You can launch a beta version of your product, with an early bird price and a whitelist. If it fails, you do not lose any credibility whatsoever. If it works, not only do you get access to valuable and immediate feedback, allowing you to improve the product or service, but you also validate the idea-market fit.

#### Know Them Better Than They Know Themselves

Identity is more than a static collection of facts. Your name, gender, age, occupation, values and beliefs intertwine to form a psychological and physical landscape named “You.” It is not static either, it is akin to a dynamic navigation system that continuously adapts and responds to internal and external stimuli. Now add your own story to the mix and who you are gets even more complex. In order to understand someone, you need a breakdown of surface-level demographics to explore the mechanisms that drive their behavior. You are looking at three dimensions:

- Demographics.

- Psychological terrain.

- Narrative architecture.

Demographics are the entry points which allow you to understand an individual’s thinking. Simply put, people see the world differently according to their age, their gender, and their social status.

Parents tend to adopt a longer-term view while adults without children tend to be more hedonistic.

My point is, you want to look beyond a bland list of demographics and understand how they shape the psychological terrain of those people. What are their North star values? #### Make Them Chase You

In 2010, a well-established peanut butter candy bar faced declining sales. The company needed a distinctive campaign to put them back on top of their crowded market. Research conducted by their ad agency revealed a fascinating universal truth: Hunger drastically alters personality. It makes people grumpy, moody, and sometimes downright nasty. This insight sparked a revolutionary campaign that would dramatically boost sales: “You are not you when you are hungry.”

Snickers hit the nail on the head with this universal feeling. Everyone has a fun or frustrating

Core emotions (happiness, sadness, fear, disgust, anger, surprise) shape our perception and receptivity more than we care to admit. When you are not feeling well, you will be too concerned with fixing this unpleasant feeling to accept being sold to. You will shield yourself from external solicitations. You will resist.

Resistance is a communication mechanism. When someone appears resistant, they are revealing critical information about their internal psychological state. Information you can use to your advantage. When someone resists persuasion attempts, they are telling you that they still have unaddressed fears, unmet needs, and active protective mechanisms. Their tone, their words, their gestures, will signal unease. This is your cue to step back and give them some air. Never force your way through resistance, you risk losing them forever. Instead, flip the script.

Flipping the script means reversing the dynamic on its head. If you have been frequenting cats, you know that they hate people who want their attention. You know, the cuddlers, the petters, the kissers. Cats do not like clinginess. But they will be magnetized to people who actively flee from them. Your allergic friend, for instance, will be a cat’s unwilling best friend.

Consider resistance as a signal that you have lost them already. In fact, I would recommend never pushing to convince anyone. Actual persuasion and convincing—meaning having people change their minds—works through detachment. In other words, the more you try to convince someone, the less it works.

### How to Improve Your “Relationship” Reduce External Influences

When you attempt to persuade someone, your first obstacle is rarely the persuadee, but their social circle. It happens all the time. In fact, it is one of the most invoked excuses, because it transfers responsibility onto a third party. Now they do not have to feel bad because they have wasted your time! #### Create a Positive Feedback Loop

A feedback loop is the simple idea of an effect influencing its cause. You probably know the Larsen effect when you put a microphone next to its speaker. The sound reverberates, getting louder and louder, until it produces an excruciatingly loud screech. This is called a “reinforcing” feedback loop; a snowballing effect where each new iteration of the


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 157 -->

process is bigger than the last. There are also “balancing” feedback loops, where the system corrects itself to avoid catastrophic failure. For instance, when it gets too warm outside, you begin to sweat: That’s your body’s balancing feedback loop to avoid overheating. If you did not possess such an ability, you would simply become as warm as it is outside, and you would die. In the same fashion, every persuasion attempt has reinforcing and balancing feedback loops. For example, let us say you are addressing an audience to pitch them your latest course. If they are slowly dozing off, this is the sign that you are boring them and need to improve your presence. If you do not, your relationship with them will worsen. This balancing feedback loop allows you to avoid failure, if you notice it. Now, on the other hand, if you sell a course, and people love it so much that they want to buy the next one on the list, this is a great reinforcing feedback loop. Your relationship with them will be further based on trust. This is why it is much easier to sell a new product to


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 158 -->

customers than it is to sell it to strangers. Because they already know you produce good stuff!


### How to Make Their “Desire” Irrepressible Make the Desire Theirs

Imagine a big, red button which says, “Do Not Push.” What is the first thing that comes to mind? You want to press it to see what happens! No-one likes being told what to do, so when we are, we instantly think of doing the opposite. This is especially the case when it goes against our beliefs.

Conversely, we are ready to die for our own ideas, principles, and aspirations. We create a sense of ownership around ideas that may not be ours in the first place. This happens because we have a deep-seated need for autonomy and self-determination. When people want something for themselves, the worst thing you can do is prevent them from having it or tell them this thing they crave is not for them. Just like the red button example, they will go with the


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 159 -->

opposite reaction and pursue it even more. When people believe a desire is theirs, they become emotionally invested in it and defend it passionately. They integrate it into their personal narrative until it becomes a part of their identity.

This is what you want to achieve with your persuasion attempt. Telling someone “You should want this” will never work, because you will inevitably and invariably be met with resistance. Just like you cannot force a seed to grow by yelling at it, you cannot force someone to want something.

What you can do, though, is create the conditions where it naturally flourishes. You give your plant plenty of light, water, and minerals. This is something master salesmen do beautifully. Instead of telling their prospects what they should do, they subtly guide the conversation. Their deep knowledge of the industry certainly does not make them fortune-tellers, and they do not pretend like they are. Instead, they show they have been around long enough to have a precise feel for where things are headed unless


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 160 -->

a change is made. In other words, they tell stories that resonate and share strategic pieces of information.

Instead of taking up the whole space and turning it into a classroom lecture, they engage in conversations so profound they can feel salutary at times. How? By asking questions, but not just the “open-ended” kind like most “good” salesmen do. They ask deep questions that make their prospect truly think. Questions that make them go “Wait, I did not think of it that way.” These questions have no predefined answer. They knock people off their chairs. But they move the conversation forward like nothing else. Those are the questions that make people come to their own conclusions, and in doing so, make them feel like their desire is “theirs.” Once they do, they will fight tooth and nail to make it a reality.


#### Touch Their Bleeding Wounds

Pain is a fundamental catalyst of transformation. It is not always physical. It can also be an unbearable psychological feeling which

#### Show Them That It Is Not Their Fault

Few words provide as much relief as “it is not your fault,” because guilt is one of the worst possible feelings to us. We hate to blame ourselves, yet we are our own worst critic. But in truth, fault is often more of a psychological construct than an objective reality.

The reason behind this is that we only have one point of view capable of judging unfolding events: Ours. And within our own point of view, we miss numerous objective elements, mechanisms, and circumstances, which are not accessible to us through our everyday cognition.

This is why honest people will often take the blame for things going wrong, even when it is truly not their fault. Have two honest people work together, and both will feel responsible for a failure if it does happen. Conversely, dishonest people


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 168 -->

(liars, sociopaths and psychopaths) will often look for someone else to blame.

Whatever it may be, what seems like minor setbacks from the outside can feel like catastrophic failures to us. As a result, our self-esteem suffers, we self-sabotage, and end up taking fewer risks. But in truth, this internal dialogue is a pattern of self-perception that was learned, and which can be modified.

This is where you intervene. Your role is to reframe a situation, switching its supposed cause with another more obvious one. If someone is overweight, it is not their fault. It is not because they lack discipline or self-control. In fact, they usually make tremendous efforts to lose weight. The real cause of their weight problem is the food they eat— and the food industry is responsible for putting terrible ingredients inside our everyday food. As you can see, this works not through lying, because the food industry is truly rotten to the core, but through a simple perspective shift. And since we are working


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 169 -->

with abstract concepts and situations, it works without any unethical manipulation.

Now, here is a crucial tip. The phrase “It is not your fault” has been so overused that it has lost its meaning8. I personally avoid saying it. I prefer showing people how it is not their fault, like establishing a new cause, and a new culprit, instead of being too direct about it: “You have done your best in losing weight, but the food industry will simply not let you achieve your goals. They profit off keeping Americans fat.” This works without having to use rehashed phrases which trigger their radar.

### How to Make Your “Projection” Vivid The Importance of Vision

It should be clear by now: Projection is the neurological process wherein a future reality becomes temporarily real, as if it already existed. Whether you actively build this projection, or leave this task for the reader, does not matter. Nouns recreate objects; verbs recreate actions. Any discourse using nouns and verbs will generate a


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 172 -->

projection. With that said, you can—and should— make these projections as vivid as possible.

To do so, you must understand a projection’s underlying architecture. A projection should not feel like a vague potentiality. It should feel like the thread of destiny: Inevitable. This requires 3 things: Provide enough sensory (“3D”) details to make this potential reality feel as real as possible, while simultaneously connecting the projection to deeper identity needs, so as to show how the projection changes the experience of the individual. Step 1, identify their emotional needs, assess their injuries. Identify a problem and its reverse desire. Step 2, translate this abstract longing into a concrete sensory experience. Do not be afraid of going “physical.” Smell, touch, hearing, sight… And everything in between. Then draw a bridge between these concrete—3D—feelings and sensations and their emotional counterparts. Feelings and emotions are not the same, but they are linked. Feeling energetic makes you “feel” the emotion of being invincible. Just like you had the world in the palm of your hand. One leads to another—but not


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 173 -->

always. Your job is to make this bridge clear, concrete, and as tangible as possible.

Consider the car example. Driving a great car “feels” great, in terms of sensations, as well as emotions. Speed is a feeling. The pride of driving a fast car (which tells a story of success and achievement) is an emotion. One can lead to the other. Different cars (to stick with this example) lead to different experiences. A classy sedan is a statement of good taste, a wagon signals you are a responsible and grounded family man, a pickup truck means you are an outdoorsman.

Knowing this nails down the fact that any experience goes beyond its materialistic components, just like a car is much more than an engine, metal, and wheels. In the end they are just the catalyst allowing for the sensorial, emotional, and transformative experience.

So, stop thinking that people care about features like fuel efficiency, mileage, and their counterparts in any type of product. These features only matter in certain circumstances, where the feature is an


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 174 -->

essential (meaning irreplaceable) part of the desired experience. A quartz watch and a mechanical watch will never signal the same experience, feelings, and emotions. Features matter, but they do not matter by themselves—always in relation to the final feelings and emotions they generate.


#### When to Be Specific, When to Be Vague

Crafting vivid projections means striking a fine balance between precision and possibility—too rigid and you suffocate imagination, too loose and the vision dissolves into meaningless abstraction. People do not want an exhaustive blueprint that dictates every minute detail, nor do they want generic whispers that evaporate upon touch. You want to provide just enough structural integrity to make the future feel tangible, while leaving enough expansive space for personal interpretation. This is why the greatest persuasionists are part architects, part poets. They understand that people do not want to be told exactly who they will become; they just want to be


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 175 -->

shown the possibility of transformation. Do not worry, they will fill in the details themselves.

The idea is to be ultra specific where it counts: The pillars. You, them, the problem, its linked desire, the solution, its mechanism, the dream outcome, and its costs. “Problem-desire A” leads to “dream C,” and it goes there thanks to “solution B.” You should be able to draw a straight line from A to C. When I say “specific,” I mean something identifiable. For instance, tonight you will go to sleep on your bed. Your bed is unique. It is yours and nobody else’s (except a partner, of course). In fact, it is so unique that we could give it a name. Once you name it, you differentiate it, you turn it into its own separate reality. This is “specificity.” Specific means unmistakable with something of the same category. Just like Lassie is not just any dog, not even any colley. Lassie is Lassie.

Any type of persuasive attempt which confuses desires and projections, which does not know its target, or why it even exists, is doomed to fail. You


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 176 -->

cannot know the solution to a problem if you cannot articulate it clearly.

Conversely, you should not imprison the persuadee into a rigid transformation, just because you think they will like it. Or worse, because you like it yourself. They will be the ones to judge, so let them decide for themselves. First, because you cannot know the many different and unique circumstances of their life—you can only know so much about someone. Their taste, their preferences, might be different from yours. Second, because you cannot tell them how to interpret—meaning how to feel—about the transformation you offer them. Never assume that you know, if you do not know. For instance, some projections speak to a majority of people, some do not. Some projections you can build from direct knowledge (also known as prior research), some other projections are wild guesses. If you know with certainty, be specific. If you do not, remain vague, and leave things open to interpretation. Give people space, and freedom, to build their own projection. The one which will work for them. And if you do not


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 177 -->

know? Try asking them. People will usually tell you how they would like to be sold.


### How to Make Your “Solution” Quick and Easy Explain It Through a Unique Mechanism

Focusing on the emotional transformation is not an excuse to neglect the logic behind your solution. You want to explain it like a mechanical process, a clear explanation of the transformation, and you want it to be different from all the other solutions available. In other words, unique. This is what we call the “unique mechanism.”

There are several types of mechanisms. Usually, we refer to the “unique mechanism” as the unique way your solution works, but there are other mechanisms at play in a persuasion attempt.

First, there is the commonly understood mechanism of the problem you are tackling. Usually this is an incorrect explanation people wrongly think is true. For instance, people commonly think that to


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 178 -->

lose weight, you need to eat less. And while this is somewhat true, fewer calories does not always equal weight loss, just like higher calories does not always equal weight gain. This belief is called the doxa, the commonly accepted belief of the masses, and this is the one you want to fight, by explaining how it has gotten things wrong. You do this on a level which seems technical, while making it easy to understand.

To stick with the same example, you would show how a higher metabolic rate leads to more calories being burned. Meaning that keeping your metabolism high will lead to weight loss, even while eating more food. In other words, lose fat while eating more. This is the new (correct) mechanism of the problem.

By redefining the mechanism of the problem, you open a new world where your solution is now the only one to correctly address the problem. Of course, in my example, your product is a way to upregulate anyone’s metabolism effectively and without any risk. And you will go into scientific details to show how it works inside the body—the actual unique


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 179 -->

mechanism. For instance, product X works by blocking enzyme Y, which slows down your metabolism. Without enzyme Y active, your body can now burn fat more effectively.

This changes everything. Before, everybody was competing on the same playing field: Calorie deficit. Now, you are the only true solution on the market.

Here is another real example. In the 1960s, when nutrition knowledge was uncommon, it was easy to have you believe that dietary fat was stored as body fat, and that you would get lean by simply eating less of it. The law of thermodynamics made sense too: Move more and the fat will melt like butter in a hot pan.

But as the field of nutrition science progressed, and as current explanations were shown to be ineffective, new mechanisms had to be introduced. Some people started looking at carbohydrates instead of fats and it was not long before they started to point their finger at it. “The real reason we have so many obese people is because we eat too much sugar! Our


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 180 -->

ancestors did not have access to this much! We need to get back to our ancestral ways of eating!” And just like that, society started demonizing sugar and initiated the low-carb diet craze. Keto, paleo, carnivore, became the new best-selling weight loss solutions on the market, because they introduced new mechanisms, like replacing sugar with “better sugars” like xylitol, erythritol, or stevia. But now they work less, because awareness evolves. Right now, we are in the middle of pointing our finger at yet another “bad guy”: Seed oils. And so begins a new cycle with its brand-new solutions.


#### Make Them Eat the Cherry Before the Cake

The hard truth about persuasion is that a quick partial solution will almost always beat a more comprehensive, but slower, transformation. People do prefer instant results over lengthy processes. This does not mean you should jump to make every product you create an immediate solution, especially if this leads you to manipulation or false promises. If


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 181 -->

you can, you should, but some problems do take time to solve, and in these cases, you do not have a choice. Instead, you need to understand how real change happens in the human mind: Every small win creates momentum, which motivates us to keep going towards bigger ones. Comprehensive transformations are simply an aggregate of multiple smaller (and more immediate) wins.

I have seen this with two nearly identical joint health supplements. One contained a superior, patented ingredient, meant to rebuild cartilage over six months, while the second combined an immediate pain relief with a more long-term rebuilding of the joint matrix. Although less impressive, the second product dominated the market because its quick initial relief gave people a reason to stick around for the deeper transformation. In other words, one quick win leads to another, and this accumulation of wins builds a larger, and deeper, transformation. All the first supplement brand (the one with the patented ingredient) would have to do to improve their marketing, would be to fractionate


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 182 -->

its larger result into a series of clear, identifiable, steps: First week, first month, second month, etc.

Making your solution quick does not mean only working on the first results it provides. Rather, it is about making is progressive, where instead of having one big effect, and then nothing, you get a series of new, consistent, results, so that the user always has something for which to wait and strive.

The most successful coaching programs do this wonderfully: They offer in-depth training over several weeks or months, but they make sure to structure it in a way where students begin by acquiring new clients and signing new contracts. The “toughest” problem gets solved first, generating self-sustainable cashflow and new, more “agreeable” problems to solve. This keeps students thirsty for more and makes it so they can achieve longer-term results without asking any questions. For coaches, this increases life-time value and prevents churn.

This proclivity towards quick gains is the reason why ketogenic diets for weight loss have been all the rave in the past few years. Can you guess what


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 183 -->

happens in the first few hours of limiting sugar? Your body rids itself of excessive water weight, resulting in a visibly less “puffy” look in as little as 48 hours. Despite the restrictive nature of this diet, these almost instantly visible results (compared with more traditional weight loss approaches) motivate you to keep going, because you are seeing with your own eyes that it does, indeed, work! Now, guess what happens after a few more days? Your muscles start to deplete themselves of glycogen, which are stored carbohydrates… And voilà, you have lost a couple kilos—or more—in just a few days! This will in turn motivate you to stick with the diet to reap its full benefits.

This principle has transformed how I present solutions. Nowadays, I structure the mechanism to deliver its fastest measurable effect first. For instance, while a business coaching program cannot guarantee a new client next week, it can certainly secure at least one prospect call, through an aggressive outbound plan of action and done-for-you cold email scripts. Rather than starting with voice


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 184 -->

training, a public speaking course can open with a simple posture adjustment that instantly makes speakers feel more confident, which can be verified through video recording on the first session.

A meditation app can lead with physical relaxation before addressing mental clarity. Users would first learn to release the tension in their neck and shoulders, which they would feel within minutes. This immediate result makes them more receptive to the deeper practice that follows. The key is in identifying the “low hanging fruit” in your solution: Which part creates the fastest verifiable change? This becomes your opening move, even if it is not your most important effect. You are simply reordering your solution for maximum impact.

Here is another thing you can do: Break down your transformation into distinct phases, each with its own “victory.” For instance, a dating coaching program can structure its 90-day transformation into clear weekly wins: Week 1 focuses on simple conversation techniques with the simple goal of keeping a talk going without feeling awkward. Week


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 185 -->

2 introduces body language adjustments that clients can verify through recorded interactions. Week 3 moves to specific dating app strategies that measurably increase their number of “matches.” Here, each phase builds confidence while moving towards the larger goal of getting into a relationship. A guitar course would not start with general music theory. They would teach you your first simple, recognizable song straight away. Next, you would learn your first solo riff and build towards completing your first song. As you can see, these phases do not detract from the deeper transformation, they build towards it while implementing clear wins along the way, so as to keep people engaged with the solution.

Next, you want to make the results explicitly measurable in the shortest possible timeframe. Look back at our business coaching program example from earlier to see it in action. Stay away from vague promises like “deeper sleep.” Promise “You will fall asleep 15 minutes faster tonight after you try this specific technique the Navy SEALs use on the


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 186 -->

battlefield.” The key is in identifying what can be measured immediately, not just what should be measured eventually. This gives concrete proof your solution works, immediately after they start interacting with it.

Meanwhile, do not lose sight of the bigger picture: Position slower transformations as “background processes” happening alongside the quick wins. For instance, an executive coaching program could write: “In your first week, you will master three communication techniques that immediately command more respect in meetings. Meanwhile, your leadership presence is developing in the background, like compound interest. Each small interaction builds your reputation until one day, people suddenly realize they have been treating you differently for weeks.” A skincare line might explain: “You will notice softer skin texture within hours. Meanwhile, deeper cellular renewal begins immediately and only takes 30 days to become fully visible.” You want to paint a clear picture of both timelines: The quick wins that keep people engaged


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 187 -->

and the deeper transformations quietly unfolding in the background. This satisfies both the need for instant gratification and long-term change.

People do not give up on effective solutions. They quit before experiencing results, and most often because of a lack thereof. So, by designing—and presenting—your solution to deliver meaningful victories as quickly as possible, you will be solving one of the biggest challenges of persuasion: How to keep people engaged long enough for the real transformation to happen.


#### Make It New

Some people say “New” is the most powerful word in advertising. I would not go as far, but I concede it is one of the most powerful words when it comes to persuasion. The human brain is constantly processing information, categorizing experiences into known frameworks, filtering out what seems familiar, and novelty disrupts this automatic sorting system. Now the key to making novelty work is not in creating something entirely unrecognizable but


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 188 -->

introducing a subtle twist that makes people’s brain pause, recalibrate, and lean in with curiosity.

But here is the catch: the most powerful novelty does not kick through the door like a SWAT team looking for a pet squirrel, nor does it announce itself with grandiose claims. Instead, it presents itself through an unexpected reframing, transforming its audience’s perception just enough to make them feel both comfortable and intrigued. It feels revolutionary and intuitive at the same time, because our brains have developed survival mechanisms that make us pay attention to both opportunities and threats. The art of novelty lies in understanding that, while people want breakthroughs, they do not want truly radical change. They want familiar challenges presented through an unexpected lens that makes the solution feel both surprising and natural.


#### Keep It Safe

The human brain is a prediction machine constantly scanning its environment for potential dangers. We are biased against threats—which


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 189 -->

translates into us being more sensitive to potential losses than potential gains (i.e., people do not purchase insurance for the benefit of having coverage but to avoid potential catastrophic loss). So not only do you want to make your solution feel inevitable, but you also want it to feel secure. Every potential transformation triggers an unconscious risk assessment that wants to default to preservation. We do not resist change per se, we resist the potential catastrophic consequences of that change. Your job is to systematically anticipate fears before they crystallize and dismantle them.

Now here is where it gets tricky: Fear is not a gigantic monolith, but a collection of micro-anxieties that add up. When it comes to investments for instance, some people fear losing money, others fear social judgment, and a tiny percentage fear missing out on opportunities. Your persuasion attempt must address these dimensions and create what could be seen as a “transformation corridor” where going forward feels natural and secure. But you do not eliminate risk either—you reframe it. And the way


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 190 -->

you do it is by providing a clear and predictable pathway (more on that in a minute). You provide evidence that remaining static is riskier than moving forward. In other words, the cost of inaction is greater than the potential transformation you are offering. You will never remove all danger, but you can make the journey feel so well engineered, the path so well trodden that the potential reward dramatically outweighs perceived threats.

Safety is closer to an emotional experience than a logical calculation. While guarantees can support your persuasion effort, making people feel understood, protected, and guided will yield the best results. “I get your fears but fret not as I have already solved them” is the message you want to tell. And when you do, transformation becomes the safest and most comfortable option.


#### Make It Natural and Somewhat Effortless

The best solutions feel natural. That is because nature, as a concept, refers to a divine process. A process is an ever-ongoing transformation. When


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 191 -->

you understand that, you realize why people prefer natural over synthetic every single time. Because nature is ordered, safe, and certain. Anything natural flows like water from a stream, whereas anything synthetic feels forced and nefarious (just look at how people shun food additives.)

Natural is easier, and anything easier is preferable in general. Most people do not want to be told they must “climb a mountain” to achieve their goals. They prefer being guided along a waymarked trail with safety lines and having sherpas carry their bags. This is why people pay good money for done-for-you services. To save time and energy! So, unless they are looking for effort, which happens in certain niches, effortless solutions prevail over complex ones.

Now, you still want to strike a good balance between easy and rewarding. Let me give you an example to illustrate. When Duncan Hines introduced their cake mix in the 1950s, they designed them to be extremely simple: Users only needed to add water, mix, and bake. But despite the


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 192 -->

convenience, the product did not sell well. The manufacturer hired a psychologist to investigate why, and what he discovered turned it into a classic case study of marketing psychology. It turns out that housewives were feeling guilty about the extreme simplicity. They felt like they were not “baking” because the process was too easy. In fact, they felt like they were cheating! The solution was brilliant in its simplicity: The manufacturer adjusted the recipe to require the addition of fresh eggs to the cake mix. And just like that, with one extra step, housewives felt like they were genuinely contributing to their family’s meal. They could feel proud of baking a “homemade” cake while enjoying the convenience of a mix.

Let this be a lesson for you: Make your solution natural, quick and easy, while making people feel like they are actively participating.


#### Keep It Boringly Predictable

Humans are pattern-seeking creatures. We are hardwired to look for opportunities, threats, and


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 193 -->

expected outcomes. In other words, we project probable events before engaging in an action.

This is why so few people get into entrepreneurship: Its unpredictable nature is a red flag for our lizard brain. We want to know the destination, but also the precise route which will get us there… And if we can avoid any surprises on the way—that would be even better.

This is how your solution should feel: Tried and tested. Boring, reliable, reproducible. They must feel like hundreds of thousands of people have been in their shoes before, and that it went the same every single time.

To do this well, use candid presuppositions. Presuppositions are naive assumptions that make the desired outcome feel like a totally normal conclusion, as if you had seen it hundreds of times before. For instance, to sell a weight-loss program you could say something like “I cannot be held responsible for the new clothes you will have to buy.” If you had to sell a hair growth treatment, you could say: “Be aware that your hairdresser budget


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 194 -->

may increase in the next few months of using this product.” This approach lets you do several things at once:

- It assumes success is inevitable.

- It implies transformation is so significant that

a second order of action is necessary.

- It shifts the conversation from “if” to “when.”

Effective presuppositions are conversational in their tone, you want them to feel natural and not forced. It should feel like a quick and innocent piece of advice.


### How to Make the “Action” Direct and Painless Break the Process into Easy Steps

All our decisions are based on a subconscious calculation. “How much energy will I have to spend to get my reward?” Actions deemed too challenging, or not worth the effort, will be met with resistance. Friction comes from multiple sources: Being afraid


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 195 -->

of failing, the uncertainty of winning, and the perceived complexity of the task ahead, which may reveal inaptitude. And of course, social judgment. All friction is psychological in nature. You can make your solution as simple as pushing a button, but what matters in the end is how others perceive it. What is easy to you may seem insurmountable to others. Look at bodybuilders, for instance. To them, weighing all of their food is just a part of cooking, an additional step that does not even register as effort. But ask a random person to do it and it will most certainly seem like a huge pain in the neck.

In order to reduce psychological friction, deconstruct these invisible barriers. Make acting easier than doing nothing. Here are some practical ways to do it:

- Break everything into the smallest possible

steps. Going to the gym starts with getting

your gym clothes ready, which demands

much less effort than exercising.

- Remove the parts that require decision-

making to reduce cognitive effort.


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 196 -->

- Create a clear path with minimal uncertainty.

- Make the feedback loop as short as possible,

add small rewards along the transformation.

Anticipate! Dissolve resistance before it takes its full shape, because once it does, moving them will be an unsurmountable task. Make the action required by your solution feel natural and effortless. This is exactly what “1-click buying” achieved, with massive returns. It made the painful part of filling out your payment information disappear. Psychologically, it does not feel like you are buying anything. You are just clicking on a button. Well, this is exactly the idea. Think of pulling the individual toward performing the action, instead of pushing them toward it.


#### Show Them a Clear Path Ahead

Humans want to feel safe when they make a decision. They want it to feel natural and comfortable. A clear—yet suboptimal—decision will always prevail against a better but more confusing one.


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 197 -->

You can witness this by looking at people around you. Most of them follow a very similar path. They got a degree, landed a secure job, got a mortgage, moved in with their partner, had children, and retired. While this may not be the best way to live one’s life, it is a straightforward path. At each step of the way, you have a pretty good idea of what comes next. You know the expectations, the challenges, the rewards. And this is why this lifestyle “works.”

People do not resist the action in itself; they resist their own perception of its difficulty. If the action required to make your solution work is seen as difficult, uncertain, uncomfortable, or unclear… No matter how good the outcome is, people will dismiss your solution. The brain works this way: If I do not instantly “get” what you mean, and what is coming next, I will dismiss the whole thing. When you sell a product, an idea, or a project, tell them exactly what you expect from them. Tell them that they need to click on the link, tell them about the order form they will need to fill out, tell them about the confirmation


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 198 -->

email they will receive, tell them how long the product will take to land in their mailbox. What may seem obvious to you is not obvious to thousands of people. Never assume that something is obvious. Prefer clarity, even if it may feel redundant, to assuming anything.


#### Validate Their Choice All Throughout

Humans crave immediate gratification. Quick results. Instantaneous wins. And you can use this scientific fact to your advantage. Even a tiny, measurable result, or confirmation, is enough to trigger a powerful neurological response. This is how our brain is wired. Add to every step of the persuasion process initial glimpses of transformation. They create a dopamine-fueled feedback loop that makes subsequent steps very attractive. Start, for instance, by validating their choice. Have they bought from you? Congratulate them. Tell them they have made a good choice. Tell them that they are different—special. Make them feel good and keep everything genuine and elegant.


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 199 -->

Encourage every positive action, no matter how small, with a reward. Reward opening your emails, reading them, buying your products, etc. with attention, care, surprise gifts, etc. Establish positive reinforcement, where engaging means being rewarded.

Engineer their experience so they go from quick win to quick win as soon as possible. They must keep on winning all the time when they engage with you.


### How to Increase or Decrease the “Costs” Make the Positive Costs Tiny and the Negative Ones Huge

A friend working in sales offered one of his clients a chance to join a referral program. He told his client “You will get $200 for each sale I make thanks to your referral,” to which the client calmly replied, “I wouldn’t even get out of my chair for $200.”


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 200 -->

People evaluate opportunities based on more than their monetary implications. Said simply, price rarely matters. Everyone, depending on their own situation, has a personal threshold based on both their financial capacity and the price of their money. To some people, $200 is a lot of money, and these people could be millionaires. To others, $200 is nothing, even though they do not have as much in their bank account. As such, prices are quite elastic, and you should not fall into the trap of underpricing your products.

In the same way, people evaluate opportunities through costs other than monetary. Energy, time, difficulty, status, etc., are all currencies. And just like on foreign exchanges, each of these currencies are worth a certain amount of money—except this time, people set their value themselves. This means that, depending on the audience and the niche, the value of these currencies can be quite different. Find out what your niche thinks of them and operate accordingly. Some people are ready to spend thousands of dollars on services which buy them


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 201 -->

time. Others will want to spend as little as possible and do all the work themselves.

Once you have done that, you want to price your product or service accordingly. Make sure that the price of acting, meaning the price they will pay (in any of these currencies) to go forward with you, will be much less compared to the price of not doing anything.

Here, you want to make it feel exponentially more expensive than the investment required, whether it is money, time, or effort. Show how the accumulating cost of remaining the same snowballs into an unmanageable mess that will tip the scales at some point. Think, “Winter is coming.”9 Show that no matter how they think about it, this is an investment they will have to make, whether that is right now or years later. Make people feel like all roads lead to your solution, and the shortest one— taking action right now—is the least painful one.

9 In his book Flip the script, master salesman Oren Klaff explains that you should always show the future as harder than the present. This “Winter is coming” technique forces people to consider acting now, because the cost of inaction could be catastrophic.


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 202 -->


#### Reframe Their Fears

Fear is the invisible emotion that constrains human action. People do not resist “spending” in itself, they resist the potential psychological, social, and financial consequences of that investment. This means that your job is to transform these perceived risks into opportunities for transformation. Addressing the spending considerations is only scratching the surface when it comes to persuasion. Deep anxieties are what really drives decision-making. Are they feeling inadequate— “This is not for me”? Are they afraid they might lose more money down the road? Do they fear the social stigma of failure, or being judged by others? Maybe they are not ready for a shift in identity?

To kill these fears, you can provide clear risk mitigation strategies, like a guarantee, for instance. You can take the burden of the risk for them, and show you have “skin in the game.” You can emphasize the greater risk of inaction and shift their perspective. You can offer detailed and predictable pathways, and reinforce a sense of safety, making


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 203 -->

them feel they are “in good hands.” In any case, the most elegant fear alleviation does not argue against fear but reframes it. You want to show that your solution is the actual protective mechanism against potential bigger losses down the line. The people who hesitate about joining a fitness coaching program might be afraid of looking foolish at the gym. They may be concerned about being judged by their peers for engaging in what may seem “vain.” They may fear dramatically changing their lifestyle and ending up too different from the norm. Simple ways to alleviate their fears could be to offer private sessions in a gym studio or accompany them to their local gym. Being part of a supportive community could help with reevaluating their perception of what is normal—to some people, training every day is like brushing their teeth. Transformation case studies of people with a similar starting point could be helpful too and so would presenting the program as insurance against potential health declines.


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 204 -->


#### Reveal Hidden Costs

Some costs are more obvious than others. Some are even hidden, and people are not hardwired to anticipate them. For instance, most people have no idea that endless doomscrolling fries their brain and makes them numb to the simple pleasures of life. And if they are, they underestimate the consequences. Thus, it is your role to highlight the hidden costs of inaction. Do not focus on the immediate consequences, though. Instead, paint a vivid picture of the cascading long-term implications of maintaining their current state. You can always quantify the incremental losses over time to show the compounding effects of missed opportunities. But ideally, you want to paint a vivid picture of how staying stagnant erodes future potential. A story will always be more powerful than statistics. Here is an example.

After 5 years in the same position at his company, a friend felt like he was stagnating. So, he started to look for other opportunities and even went through an interview. While it went well, he was not


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 205 -->

entirely convinced. It is only when wrapping the interview up that everything changed. The CEO told him, “You are 33 right now, you are married without children, and if you want to become a father, chances are it will be happening in the next year or two. So, if you switch industries right now, you have a year or two ahead of you to establish the right foundations. But if you do not, you are unlikely to switch jobs when your kid is still young. Men become comfortable when they become first-time fathers. Then they usually ‘wake up’ around 45 and realize it is too late for them. They do not have the same hunger, the same energy, and now they are competing with 30-year-olds who are just as qualified as them. They become washed up, bitter, and pray they do not lose their job before retirement.” After a brief pause and a friendly stare, he concluded with “If you want to make a career move, the time is now.” And just like that, my friend decided to join his company.

The CEO cleverly showed my friend the many hidden costs of inaction. Similarly, you want to


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 206 -->

create an illumination, such as “Damn, that guy is right, I never thought of it this way!”

For it to have the desired effect (a true “wake up call”), your discourse must feel like a bucket of icy water. It must shock and make perfect sense at the same time. Do it tastefully without disrespect, insults, or covert attacks. Keep your discourse emotionally compelling, but brutally logical. The link between the inaction and its consequences must make perfect sense.

It must also be relatable. My friend was an ambitious young man, and the CEO picked up on that. He could exactly picture the “washed up, bitter, 45-year-old salesman who’s just sucking up company benefits and barely hitting quotas while waiting for retirement” because he has been around these guys. The picture was already there, but he could not see how he could end up in that place. His future employer made that connection clear for him. It had the tone of “I’ve seen it time and time again” and it worked like a charm. And just like that, he was able to transform what could have been a half-


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 207 -->

hearted transaction—“work for me in exchange of this salary”—to a strategic life choice.


#### Use Urgency—Not Pressure

Let us make things clear: Urgency is not artificial pressure. Rather, it is about showing how every choice (inaction being a choice) has irreversible consequences, and how these consequences may come sooner than people think.

The difference between urgency and pressure lies in their underlying architecture. Pressure is an external force pushing against someone’s will. While it will cause some people to act, it will feel forced, unpleasant, and will not leave them with a positive impression. As such, the chances that they willingly come back for more are limited. That is because, in their mind, you are the cause of their suffering. They will want to shoot the messenger and are unlikely to do business with you again. And because pressure naturally creates resistance, it will paralyze some people who will rather not take action at all. When


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 208 -->

this happens, it will be close to impossible for you to earn their trust again.

By contrast, urgency is a simple recognition of potential loss. It is a gravitational pull. It does not manipulate through fear (although it can trigger it). Instead, it shows the temporary nature of the opportunity, while proving it through real circumstances. The opportunity is getting away, the window is closing, and with good reason. They should decide whether it is worth it to pursue it or not. Here, hesitation should be an expensive choice.

Have another look at the previous example. The CEO could have used pressure to get an answer from my friend. He could have said, “My offer is only available until next week.” But this would have felt artificial as there was no real reason for this time limit. Why should an offer expire like this without explanation? It does not work. Similarly, he could have said, “I have another potential hire who’s interested, I need your answer before next Monday.” And while better than the first one, neither approach may have guaranteed a positive answer. They would


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 209 -->

have resulted in resistance—and a likely “no, thanks,” because my friend was not desperate for the job. Slight pressure only works when the persuadee can be moved. If he has no real reason to do so, it will not bear any fruit.

The CEO understood he was on the losing end of this power balance, so he was smart enough to highlight the incremental erosion of his potential and the compounding consequences of delayed decision-making: “It will be a lot harder for you to switch industries when you are 45.” He created a vivid projection of what will be lost by not acting now, which resonated deeply with my friend.


### How to Make Your “Message” Resonate


#### Use Direct and Indirect Persuasion

Whether you make your persuasion attempt obvious or not is context dependent. But as a general rule, being more direct tends to create psychological barriers, while being indirect tends to slip past mental


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 210 -->

defenses. The latter is not always better, however. This is so, because direct persuasion forces awareness, whereas indirect persuasion uses the unconscious. Aware persuasion works better, and quicker, because it forces a response. When someone has a runny nose and you have a clean tissue in your pocket, you simply hand it over to them and they will take it. Direct persuasion attempts necessarily elicit a yes-or-no answer. Indirect persuasion attempts do not.

Look at how great teachers and leaders communicate. They do not declare absolute truths. Like experienced salesmen, they ask deep questions that trigger internal reflection, because they understand that people are more likely to embrace ideas they believe are theirs. And if you think teachers have little to do with salesmen, think again: They may not be asking you for money by the end of their talk, but they are selling you on their ideas.

If you decide to take the indirect route, here are some techniques you can experiment with. You can use metaphors that invite personal interpretation.

Indirect persuasion also refers to persuasion without a message. To persuade someone without any attempt, there is only one way: Familiarity. #### Understand that Places Have Auras

Physical and virtual places contain an invisible emotional architecture. They trigger a network of associations that can either amplify or diminish the effectiveness of your persuasion.

A sales pitch delivered in a sterile corporate boardroom will trigger a different response compared with one delivered in a cozy Italian restaurant over a plate of gnocchi della nonna. Similarly, virtual environments influence the reach and the strength of your message. Selling on a modern and clean-looking landing page, a 2004-like

html website, or a Google Document sends a different (and often overlooked) message. Most persuasionists see these virtual media as simple tools, instead of actual places. Stones store memories and energy. As such, one place will instantly feel cozy and comfortable, while another will feel cold and even hostile. If you were to intimidate an enemy with threats, would you conduct the dealing in a dark and humid wine cellar, or a luxury penthouse on the last floor of a 5-star hotel? This example is extreme, but you can see how some marketers forget that certain audiences prefer “homemade” and sometimes ugly websites and messages over corporate and soulless ones, because the former exudes authenticity and actual “insider” confidential information. Take the field of alternative health as an example. The audience is majoritarily retired, conservative, and tends to distrust the government and health authorities. As such, they distrust anything too official, too corporate, too “beautiful.” Confidential reports, on the other end, feel exclusive, honest, and effectively “truer,” because they are stripped of the

bullshit. People do judge a book by its cover. That is why the experience you offer them shall be total, or it shall be ineffective.


#### Turn It into a Story

Every time you listen to or read a story, your brain activates mirror neurons that allow you to mentally simulate the experience. Read about someone running from a monster, and you activate similar neural pathways. Watch James Bond save the world—or get the girl, and you feel a sense of accomplishment. It is this emotional resonance which makes the story unforgettable. Tell a child not to play with matches and he will still probably try it for himself. Now, tell him about the time you burned down the living room when you were 7 years old, and he will most likely learn the lesson.

#### Mirror Their Behavior and Language

Every individual has their own linguistic patterns, rhythms, and metaphorical frameworks which reflect different experiences and worldviews. We simply do not talk, think, and behave the same way. See this as a personal world, an alternate reality where things are not always in their place. In your world, for example, Rothko may be a boring painter. In theirs, he is the best artist ever. In their reality, the

As an expert persuasionist, realize that there is no better way to persuade someone than by entering their world. You do not do this by foolishly repeating the words and patterns they use. Or worse, repeat their first name every sentence (do not ever do this). Anything too obvious appears disingenuous. Instead, use a similar vocabulary, match their tone and energy, and adopt similar sentence structures and cadences. You want to make them feel like you are “speaking the same language.” Another thing you can do is use metaphors and analogies from their professional or cultural context. Use “jargon” which matches their world. Gym buffs will understand the idea of progressive overload. Geeks will understand the concept of “overclocking.” Copywriters will understand the idea of showing, not telling. Entering their own world makes your attempt seem familiar already; anything familiar is safe and trusted. They will feel like your message originates from within their own mind.

Being genuinely interested in what they have to say, asking pertinent follow-up questions, and taking into account their doubts and worries. Concretely, they will often repeat what their prospect just said verbatim, while upping their pitch at the end to make it seem like a question. #### Find the Right Medium

In marketing and communication, the medium refers to the nature of the message. Think speech, writing, images, videos, or body language. The channel is the specific platform or tool used to deliver the message. Think of face-to-face conversation, phone call, email, or social media post. I am not dogmatic when it comes to distinguishing these two. Instead, I want to draw your attention to how the transmission method shapes your message and its reception.

Marshall McLuhan summarized this idea in his book The Medium Is the Message. In essence, it is not just about what you say, but more about how you say it. For instance, a handwritten letter creates intimacy and personal connection, while a social media post generates collective engagement and immediacy. A text message feels instant and direct, while a voicemail carries nuance through tone and pause. These media, or channels for that matter, do not just communicate information, they are an integral part of it.


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 221 -->

Television was a sociological revolution. It has influenced the arrangement of living rooms in the West and beyond. The location of the TV dictates how the room will be arranged and where the other pieces of furniture will be. And this in turn impacts more than Feng Shui—it changes the dynamic of conversations between family members. More people have dinner in front of the TV than at the dining table, which in turn influences how they relate to each other. They spend less time talking about their day, they do not know what goes on in their lives, and they slowly grow apart. It is not an overstatement to say that media dramatically shape society. Consider smartphones and the social media generation and see for yourself.

When it comes to persuasion, you want to focus on three things, in order of importance.

The first one is the “directness” of your message. Find the most direct, and quick, way to reach the persuadee. This may be obvious, but marketers often get this wrong. If you want to reach a younger audience, do not send them emails, go


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 222 -->

where they hang out, on social media. If you want to reach a local audience, get directly to their mailboxes.

The second is the “appropriateness” of your medium. If your message is of dire importance, give it the appropriate gravitas. For instance, when legendary copywriter Bill Jayme’s sent personal letters to his friends, colleagues, and family, he would write “headlines” on the letter itself. Once, he sent a thank you note to his publisher Marty Edelston. On the envelope, it read “deeply and irrevocably personal.” Marty instantly replicated this headline for a direct mail package, and it made the package a control for years. Similarly, if you wanted to wish your mother’s birthday, calling her will always be more appreciated than a text message.

The third is the “originality” of the message. If you can make it surprising, different, while keeping things tasteful, you may have yourself a winner. A sales pitch that feels aggressive on a cold call might feel inviting in a personalized video message. A boring application sent by email may catch attention


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 223 -->

when sent as a real letter. Do not reinvent the wheel, but as long as they make sense, surprising and different media will make your message resonate.

### Rewiring North Star Values

To persuade anyone, you cannot go against who they are as a human being. Each human being sees themselves playing a role inside a structured world, whose laws are immutable. North Star values work through a priori belief. A priori means something which we believe we know by simple deduction, rather than experience or data. Someone’s world is full of unproven beliefs and assumptions. A priori beliefs are general statements such as:

- “Work more, earn more.”

- “If it is natural, it is good.”

- “Correlation implies causation.”

- “Majority opinion is correct.”

- “Hard work always leads to success.”

- “Technology always improves life.”

- “History repeats itself.”

- “More information leads to better decisions.”

- “What is legal is moral.”

- “Experts are always right.”

- “More is better.”

- “Children are blank slates.”

- “Science provides absolute certainty.”

- Etc.

All of these statements you often hear, yet they are either often gross misrepresentations, or plainly false. But still—they help people think and build a world that makes sense to them. For example, if you think that science provides absolute certainty, then from this North Star value stems a bunch of conclusions and correlations, which elevate science as a quasi-religion. This has massive implications in how you live your life, which products you buy, how you use them, and the results you get. In some ways, a priori beliefs are a form of faith, and faith is hard to dismantle. This is why you should not try to change their beliefs.

Take the example of the current opioid epidemic in the US. The common a priori belief is


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 247 -->

that pain is best solved by taking opioid-based painkillers. This belief is so deeply ingrained that it forms a rigid causal chain in most people’s minds: “I suffer (cause); therefore, I need painkillers (effect).” The pharmaceutical industry has spent decades reinforcing this narrative through advertising and medical endorsements, trying to make it a universal truth, and of course, hiding the life-threatening side effects, as if they were not part of the causal chain. But anybody who has studied natural health knows that this chain is not universal; it is just the false assumption that medication is the only effective solution. In this case, you will have a tough time trying to sell people a natural painkiller alternative to opioids because their faith in medical doctors and pharmaceuticals will not budge, despite overwhelming evidence of the danger of modern allopathic medicine.

In this case, you are better off finding another battle or reducing your message’s scope so it talks only to people who know about the dangers of opioids and painkillers—and who want to explore


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 248 -->

alternatives. But this may be too little of a population. In this case, there is another way: Harnessing an actual, current desire.

Let us say you wanted to dismantle a fickle pre-existing causal chain and replace it with a new one: For example, you offer a contrarian way of replacing stocks with crypto coins. Here their North Star value is safety. You know you can sway readers, because this audience cares more about profit (the result) than the way to get it, but they will be skeptical of anything which sounds too risky, or too good to be true. How do you reconcile the two?

Well, you must first reveal the limitations of their current belief system without breaking it. For instance, you can say that while stocks provide safe returns, their growth is usually painstakingly slow, and you either need great starting capital, or thirty years of your life, to get some actual life-changing results. Some crypto coins, on the other hand, offer both relative stability and exponential gains. You can show how stocks are tied to outdated systems that rely on slow economic growth and institutional


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 249 -->

manipulation, effectively rigging the game against the small fish. You can show through simple calculations how everyday investors never get rich through stocks, if not through sheer luck. But this only comes in opposition to their North Star beliefs, which is never ideal. You are only allowed to weaken their belief if and only if you plan to strengthen it later. Let me explain.

Instead of directly challenging their North Star belief, which would be met with fierce resistance (as people define themselves through their beliefs) you will make their own belief evolve, like you were providing them with an upgraded version of that same belief. Instead of saying “You are wrong,” you say, “you are right, and…” In my example, people who trade stocks want results, but are quite risk averse. They like stocks because they are rarely volatile. So, touting the massive potential returns of crypto may scare them away, because they know that high reward also means high risk. What you can do is confirm their belief and offer them an even better form of safety, by reframing the idea itself:


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 250 -->

What if trading stocks were the least safe way to make money? As 2008 showed us, the market is rigged toward big financial institutions which, when left uncontrolled, spiral into bankruptcy. And who pays the bill? It is the small fish; the everyday Americans like you and me who have invested their life savings. And even during the so-called good years (which have become sparse), inflation makes it so your 5% of annual yield is obliterated by the ever-diminishing value of your money, which melts away every year like snow under August’s sun. Now unlike stocks, crypto operates in a decentralized ecosystem. This means your investments aren’t at the mercy of Wall Street’s whims or government policies. Instead, they are protected by the blockchain—a transparent and secure technology free from manipulation. This makes some of the coins, like Bitcoin, predictable AND ever growing. As Michael Saylor would say, since its invention, Bitcoin has only “crashed upwards,” meaning that each crash ends up with a price higher than when it started. Compare this to any stock, which is a single global crisis away from


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 251 -->

disappearing forever, and you’ve got yourself the absolute safest bet you could ever make.

Doing this allows you to align with their belief, only to hijack it completely, and frame your solution as the one true solution. One wise saying has always stuck with me: “Offer them a new resolution to an old problem.” And to do that, as Ken McCarthy puts it: “We put up our sails and let the wind take us.” Meaning that we go with the flow, not against it.

Once you see persuasion as the surreptitious way of rewiring desire to make it fit your purpose without people noticing, all the pieces of the puzzle fall into place naturally, and you have your audience in the palm of your hands, no matter who they may be.


### Building New Worlds

Amateur persuasionists lack imagination. They believe that the only way to persuade someone is through direct means; through a persuasive message from them to their audience. But this is far from the only way. Persuasion can happen directly as well as indirectly. I can tell you to do something, explain


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 252 -->

what you would gain from it, and how the process works, and maybe you would say yes. This would be your typical, straightforward, direct persuasion. But I can also do things differently, and covertly, by simply creating a world with new behaviors, new causes and new effects. This is called world-building, and it is the secret weapon of persuasion.

The first time you were introduced to world-building was when you were a child. If you used to play with friends, then you undoubtedly played role-playing games such as cops and robbers. Or cashiers and shoppers. Role-playing games work on the premise that you become someone else and thus adopt their behavior. There is this unwritten rule that you must act according to your new character. So, when you become a cop, you must play that part, chasing the robber until you get them under control. Whereas if you play the role of the robber, you must escape no matter what. According to great child psychologist Jean Piaget, this ability to pretend arises quite early, at around 2 years old, in the “preoperational stage” of cognitive development,


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 253 -->

where we start to understand symbolic thinking. This is also when we start to respond to stories, because stories work the same way as pretend play. Storytelling creates a new momentary world with new rules, new characters, new events, new conclusions. And these rules can be very different from ours, take for instance fantasy or science fiction, where new original forms of life meet magical powers and mind-boggling technology. Yet we still believe these stories, and engage with them, because of something called the suspension of disbelief, which develops in the later stages of childhood when we become able to cognitively separate reality from fiction without a doubt, while still being able to fully immerse ourselves in both, as if we maintained a constant dual consciousness.

Worlds and their storytelling vary from quite exotic, far away from everyday reality and its immutable rules, to quite close, so close in fact that we may mistake it for reality. The latter type of stories can be seen as manipulation attempts, or even plain lies, because they are seldom revealed for what


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 254 -->

they are. They serve a purpose which, of course, might benefit their author. Gaslighting, for instance, is an abusive form of storytelling. If you do not remember, gaslighting comes from the movie Gaslight, where a husband wants to get rid of his wife by sending her to a mental facility. But to do so, he must first make her doubt her own sanity. So, throughout the movie, Gregory makes her question her reality by, for instance, manipulating the gas lights in the house, before assuring Paula that she is imagining it. Gregory wants Paula to believe the story he is constructing around her, because it would lead her to believe that she is in fact crazy. New causes, such as imagining things, lead to new effects, such as having lost your mind. This is the power of stories: They create new worlds along with their new causal orders. As long as I can get my audience to agree on the premises of my story, I can control their behavior.

Does this sound familiar? This is the purpose of hypnosis. When you are hypnotized, it happens slowly, and often with your approval. The


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 255 -->

hypnotherapist starts with premises, such as “you are sitting comfortably, your muscles completely relaxed,” and if you accept these premises, then you will have to accept their conclusions—you will have to act how relaxed people act: Your mind will slow down, you will feel more tired, you may even slowly nod off to sleep.

Now imagine that you would like to sell a whole array of products, but do it differently, using world-building techniques. Well, you could create a world where the reader plays a role. Of course, you would make this role fun and desirable, such as a secret agent, a lawyer, an adventurer… The key is to give people a role with a finality: Secret agents must succeed in their mission. Lawyers must defend their clients. Adventurers must find the treasure. That’s the definition of the role. Now, just align your products with their role, and their role with the nature of your business. For instance, if you wanted to sell products about persuasion, you could paint the reader as a secret agent, who must buy a rare and mysterious book about persuasion on the black market, because


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 256 -->

it is the key to bringing down a corrupt agency. Both reality and fiction would then align on a single goal: Buy the book, because it is simultaneously the key to learning about persuasion and accomplishing the mission. In this new world, buying makes logical sense, because it is the new effect of the causes you have laid out for them.

Do you remember Tamagotchis? The little virtual creatures you could adopt. To keep them alive, you had to feed them, clean them, and give them attention. This worked well because it recreated a common role—being a pet owner. In real life, not feeding your pets, which costs a lot of money, has dire moral and legal consequences. Pet owners must pay for pet food, shelter, and veterinary care. Adopting a pet means, 100% of the time, having to spend money on various products. It is quite a lucrative industry. So, what about recreating the same dynamic, but with virtual animals? The virtual owner would feel a similar sense of responsibility toward their virtual pet. This is what Tamagotchis got right: 40,000,000 people indeed started to spend


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 257 -->

hours tending to their virtual companions, just like they would with real ones. But the company missed the mark by not introducing additional transactions (such as virtual food, virtual toys for your pets, virtual veterinary care). If they had, they would have made an enormous profit, because virtual pet owners, just like their real counterparts, know that taking care of a pet means spending money.

World-building goes much further than this example. The idea is to recreate a complete world, with new rules, laws, and interpretations. In real life, something could be deemed right; well, in this new world you are building, it is now categorically wrong, or vice versa. You decide, because this is your world and your rules. You are a sort of demi-god in this new universe. The only challenge you have is to get people to agree with its premises. Get them to accept them, and everything will fall into place. To do so, start with what they know. Start with a simulation of reality and slowly bend the rules. Then, turn it into a game, a captivating story, and make your proposition fun and exciting. Compel


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 258 -->

them to engage with it and keep rewarding them the more they do.

### Building A World of Projection: How to Create Complete Realities That Transform Minds and Behaviors

Anyone can paint an attractive picture of the future. Real art lies in creating complete projections that flow naturally from desire to action. This requires understanding how to build new worlds where your solution is not only appealing but is the only viable choice.

➢ For a projection to work, it must flow without resistance or doubt. Most persuasion attempts fail because they mix incompatible elements, like trying to solve an immediate problem (being locked out of your house) with a long-term transformation (becoming a better person). Instead, match your projection's scope to your prospect's immediate reality, then gradually expand it to encompass bigger transformations. ➢ Every element in your projected world must connect logically to the next through clear


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 260 -->

causality. A projection fails when there are gaps in the chain of cause and effect. It is not enough to promise that your solution leads to success, you must show exactly how each step inevitably leads to the next, making the entire transformation feel both natural and certain. ➢ The secret to persuasion lies in working with your prospect's North Star values rather than against them. Never try to change someone's fundamental beliefs. Instead, evolve these beliefs by showing how your solution represents an upgraded version of what they already hold true. Do not say “you are wrong,” say “you are right, and here is an even better way to be right.” ➢ The most powerful form of persuasion happens through world-building: Creating complete realities with their own rules, behaviors, and outcomes. Like children playing make-believe, people willingly step into new roles when the world you build feels compelling. Your solution should feel like the natural law of this new world, not an external force being imposed.


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 261 -->

➢ Your projection must feel more real than current reality. This does not happen through exaggeration or hype, but through precise, vivid detail that makes your world feel more substantial than their current one. Every sensory detail, emotional resonance, and logical connection must reinforce the stability of this new world. ➢ Rewriting causality itself is the ultimate goal. In your prospect's current world, certain actions lead to certain results. In your projected world, new actions lead to better results through mechanisms that feel both novel and certain. Do not lie. Instead, reveal deeper truths about how reality works. Truths they were not aware of.

In the next chapter, you will learn how to blend subtle and dramatic persuasion techniques, creating a perfect balance that makes your message both impossible to ignore and trustworthy.

## Chapter VI: Quiet and Loud Persuasion

“A good persuasionist is a mechanic, an electrician, and an architect at the same time.”

A good persuasionist is a mechanic, an electrician, and an architect at the same time. He understands the mechanics of desire, how to rewire its circuits, and how to design and build a world where money flows directly to his bank account. But there are two distinct ways of achieving this: One is brash, arrogant, and sometimes dishonest. I call this type of persuasion, “loud.” The other is elegant, somewhat sly, but strives to remain honest. You have guessed it; this is quiet persuasion.

Loud persuasion works through an interesting inversion, which every persuasionist should study, because it sells (albeit only once per customer). It makes long-term desires pass as immediate ones and turns complex desires into quick fixes.


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 263 -->

Miracle pills sell a long-term transformation, and make it seem instantaneous. This is absurd, as even losing a few pounds involves many physiological processes: Energy balance and metabolism, fat mobilization (lipolysis), hormonal regulation (e.g., ghrelin, leptin, insulin), neuro-hormonal feedback loops, metabolic adaptation, muscle efficiency and mitochondrial function, etc. When you know even a little bit of biology and physiology, “losing 23 pounds in two weeks” makes no sense, because it equals 85,500 calories, and a single pill cannot create this much deficit. And if it did, it would probably kill you as well. But why do people still buy into ads which promise this? It all comes down to a few human traits, which we call the 7 deadly sins: Lust, gluttony, greed, sloth, wrath, envy, and pride, to which I would add ignorance.

People endlessly desire the things they lack (and they cannot help it, as it is engraved in our human condition). They regularly take more than necessary when they can. They resent their neighbor for having it better than them. They want the easy


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 264 -->

way out. They feel absolutely special and unique even though they are not. This is undoubtedly our current universal condition, and all skilled persuasionists know that you can play on these weaknesses. Thanks to Kyle Milligan who has formally theorized this fact, they also know that people respond to anything which evokes novelty, easiness, safety, and importance.

Novelty activates our opportunistic side, which loves thrill and discovery. We can link this to lust and greed. Easiness activates sloth because we do not want pain and obstacles in our way. Safety echoes our need for reliability and our fear of change. And importance simply highlights the fact that we will not settle for anything less than everything we can get our hands on: The definition of greed. Yet these are not “sins” as much as they are fundamentally biological traits. All which life desires. And there is nothing to do about it except praying for salvation. This does not make persuasion, or sales, a fundamentally corrupt activity. In fact, talking, or writing, no matter the subject, is a form of


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 265 -->

persuasion. When you use words, you attempt to recreate the world through projections. When I say, “This table is pretty,” I create a world where that specific table, the one I have in front of me, is pretty. I impose this truth on you, and you can decide to either accept my proposition, or reject it. If you agree, we have just created a shared reality. So, every time you talk, you try to crystallize the world by offering an immutable version, like you would turn it into stone. But projections are not reality. They are its shadows. And this is why Cratylus, a staunch defender of the Heraclitean vision of the world as flux, stopped talking altogether. He simply believed that words will always fail to become anything real. Words will always be the ghost of real objects.

Persuasion is not fundamentally wrong, because we both know that it can, and should, lead to a betterment of reality, as it does have concrete effects. It changes lives. It changes behaviors. You can convince someone to better himself, his health, his finances, and his relationships, through your products and your messages. And in this case, quiet


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 266 -->

persuasion will use some of the same techniques as loud persuasion, because they are effective, but with two main differences: It will not clash with the North star values of the persuadee, and it will offer a “real” causal chain, meaning that it will keep any promise it makes.

Quiet persuasion does not need grandiose claims or rely on screams for attention, because it does not need to. Instead, it simply captures the audience’s North star, and aligns its product, and its message with it. It works on the simple principle that people will never go against themselves. If I align myself with you in good faith, rejecting me means rejecting yourself. Quiet persuasion does not need claims, because it relies on a strong and clear chain from the desire (the problem), and its projection, to its materialization. For instance, a peak example of quiet persuasion is harnessing real events. Let us say that you knew, through your connections, that on a specific date, an important event would happen. A political analyst close to the White House tells you that a war is brewing in the Middle East, and that the


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 267 -->

chance of an open conflict is high. Or a tech scientist reveals to you that Microsoft is on the brink of a tremendous discovery which could change the face of the world. You could simply use this event as the starting point of your argumentation, simply laying out its multiple implications. You could even create a product designed to do exactly that, with great financial returns. In these two cases, the challenge would not be to create any type of hype, nor would it be to shout about the many (obvious) benefits of this opportunity. The mere existence of such events would be enough to convince anyone. The challenge would be to prove that they will happen when you say they will, beyond any doubt. In this case you would simply make sure that the projection you offer them aligns with both reality and their own North star.

This would allow you to convince anyone in your audience (provided that earning more money is something that interests them) without ever having to risk your skin promising outcomes and specific numbers, which would both get you in trouble with


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 268 -->

your audience and regulatory bodies. Of course, it is obvious that finding true events is the biggest challenge of them all, and the reason why quiet persuasion is not the default option. Loud persuasionists want money now, with the least amount of work necessary.


### Merging Horizons

Quiet persuasion works because unlike loud persuasion, it does not seek to impose a fake idea of reality on its readers. There will never be a world where a weight-loss pill makes anyone lose 23 pounds in two weeks, whether we like it or not. If loud persuasionists try to get people to accept these blatant lies (and if some people believe them!), just imagine how many subtler lies we have been conditioned to believe in as well. Instead, quiet persuasion strives to fuse two horizons together. This idea, developed by Hans-Georg Gadamer, is quite simple. There is reality on the one hand, the objective and scientific plan of existence, which we can partially access through our senses and our intellect.


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 269 -->

Then, on the other hand, there are horizons, points of view and subjective appreciations of reality shaped by our history, culture, language, and experiences. Mental horizons are, just like their physical counterparts, limited in scope: You cannot see what you cannot see, just as you do not know the extent of what you do not know. There are worlds and universes out there, out of the field of our consciousness, in a strange “elsewhere;” Heartbreak, raging wars, shadowy institutions, fake prophets, and tempestuous passions. The persuasionist’s role is to elevate this horizon, just like a giant ladder would, so that it reaches farther, for the persuadee to see, feel, and understand what was hitherto unknown.

Another image I like to give is the blind spot mirror, the one you usually see at tight intersections. A quiet message is like a blind spot mirror: It reveals to you a hidden reality, which helps you avoid crashing into another coming car. Loud persuasion would be akin to screaming at you that there is another car coming your way, without ever letting you come to this conclusion yourself. Just showing


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 270 -->

the blind spot, without ever having to say a single word out loud, will work as well, if not better.


### The Flexible Message

Quiet persuasion has another quality, which loud persuasion does not. Loud persuasion is fixed and immutable. It declares, it decretes, and you are either in, or out (and if you are out, shame on you!) Quiet persuasion is not this obvious to spot, because it is always evolving and adapting to its target’s movements. Just like a well-crafted garment, it fits its wearer and not the other way around; just like water, it adapts to its vessel.

This is one of my pet peeves with modern thinking, its absolute inability to think in dynamics. People, even so-called experts and intellectuals, want the world to fit their mind, whereas it should be the opposite. You simply cannot approach the world with dogma, unless you want to endlessly crash into the walls of failure and rejection. All ideas have their place and context. Some are almost never useful, some are useful sometimes, and some almost always.


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 271 -->

Quiet persuasion never settles on one approach, one method, one angle. It shapeshifts according to the persuadee and his own personal situation. It understands that you cannot persuade anyone to do anything, but that you can persuade anyone to do something. It is your responsibility to build around this thing, whatever it is.

Your message is, then, always situational. It must remain specific, meaning linked to a very precise causal chain. You want a very specific “them,” with a very specific desire, to act in a very specific way to reach a very specific projection. Like math, there is no place for guessing or wishful thinking, only precise and directed action. Your message thus adapts to your audience and its own awareness of their desire and emotional dispositions. You simply cannot sell a new Chanel handbag to a grieving mother, just like you cannot sell a car to someone without a driver’s license. Because your time would be better spent selling these products to people who want or need them.


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 272 -->

The result of this adaptation is the smoothening of the selling process, which does not feel forced or coerced—instead it becomes natural, just like a turbine channels the wind to transform it into electricity. Lifelong customers feel in control and free to actualize their desires, expand their horizons, and become who they strive to become.


### Beyond Engineering

Until now, I have shown you a mostly rationalist, and realist, approach to persuasion. But this is more of a work ethic, than an absolute profession of faith. Yes, a good persuasionist is a mechanic, an electrician, and an architect at the same time, but good mechanics, electricians, and architects are all artists. And what makes an artist is intuition: The ability to listen and trust ideas which may not come from entirely rational sources. In the context of marketing and copywriting, intuition is the “sixth sense” you build all throughout your career. It is a subtle understanding of everchanging energies, contexts, moods, and beliefs. A copywriter with


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 273 -->

intuition knows more about their audience than they themselves do. It is an interesting mix of flair (the capacity to know without proof) and vision (the idea of advancing towards a greater purpose)—two invisible forms of understanding.

When you truly understand your audience, you grasp who they are, but also when they are. In history, each era has shaped human desire in ways individual psychology alone cannot explain. For instance, promises like “work from anywhere and travel the world like a digital nomad” resonated powerfully before 2020. But once lockdowns hit, these same promises suddenly triggered anxiety rather than desire. The winners during this time were not those who pushed harder with outdated messages, but those who adapted to the new Zeitgeist—the spirit of the times—like “build a crisis-proof income with an online business that thrives in any economy.”

Think of collective consciousness as the water fish swim in. Your prospect exists beyond his personal circumstances. Global economic fears,


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 274 -->

health anxieties, and technological disruptions shape his desire. In fact, they shape entire markets, and the best companies are those who know how to ride the waves. The same supplement might emphasize protection in the year when diseases are the main concern, then switch to cost-saving prevention when inflation soars. The solution remains the same, only its projection evolves to match the current collective focus. While this was obvious in the supplements market, a similar shift happened in personal development. Programs that once sold “10X your success” suddenly felt tone-deaf in 2020. The leaders adapted, not by changing their content, but by reframing it around resilience and anti-fragility: “build unshakeable momentum in uncertain times.” You could see it with investing too. Nobody cares about “passive income” during market crashes. They much prefer “recession-resistant strategies”. Same principles, different contexts.

Each market has its own seasonal cycles. Every gym leverages the “New Year, New You” messaging, which becomes tone-deaf by March.


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 275 -->

They ramp up their marketing around June, to take advantage of the plethora of casual gym-goers who believe they can achieve their ideal summer body through a few weeks of restrictive eating and an unsustainable cardio routine. As summer ends, they ramp up their marketing one last time around September, to take advantage of the start of the new school year and to appeal to those who were appalled at how they looked in their swimsuits.

Markets also react to new trends. For instance, when CrossFit gained massive traction, traditional gyms reframed their “safe, controlled workouts” as a strength rather than a limitation. Similarly, in supplement markets, every year a new trendy ingredient comes out. If you manage to be among the first few brands to sell it, you will take most of the cake, while latecomers will only get the crumbs.

You will witness similar shifts in lots of different markets. When dating apps first appeared, traditional matchmaking services struggled. Until they reframed “old-fashioned” dating as “authentic connection.” When dating app fatigue set in, they


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 276 -->

didn't change their service, only their message: “Find real love in a world of endless swiping.” In the coffee industry, local cafés faced extinction when Starbucks expanded. The winners were not those who tried to copy Starbucks, but those who reframed “small” as “artisanal.” When third-wave coffee culture emerged, Starbucks had to adapt, shifting from “premium coffee” to “everyday luxury.” Now, with remote work culture, the same cafés are reframing themselves as “your other office.” In the positioning game, the winners are those who can zag when others zig.

To develop “contextual intuition”, you must understand three levels of timing.

First, there is the macro context: major events and shifts that affect everyone. 2020 has changed how people view health, work, and connection. For some people, it has also changed how they view the government and supra-national institutions. Macro contexts induce sudden shifts in global markets. New ways rise, old ways die. But this change is not random. Any major shift creates what we call an


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 277 -->

Overton Window: A window of opportunity for a new set of ideas to express themselves. Visionaries are people who can predict, and bet on, the opening of Overton Windows. For instance, anyone who bet on the internet before 1999, or on cryptocurrencies before 2016.

Second, there are market contexts: Cycles and changes specific to a field. They can be seasonal and recurring, or they can result from how the market matures and evolves as it becomes more competitive and sophisticated. For instance, a marketing coach who once sold “Facebook Ads Mastery” may evolve to “TikTok Shop Affiliate Supremacy” as new platforms take the center stage. A photographer hosting workshops may go from teaching professional DSLR techniques to how to use your smartphone to take better, everyday pictures.

Third, there are personal contexts: Cycles and changes in a person’s life. An aggressive investment strategy may attract a high-earning professional early in his career, but it will seem less appealing once he starts a family. By then, he will probably be more


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 278 -->

interested in protecting his assets and planning his legacy. The same holds true for fitness goals. Someone who once wanted visible six-pack abs may evolve to simply wanting to “feel strong and capable” to “stay active for his kids.” Even in luxury markets, the same buyer who once sought exclusive and eye-catching status symbols often shifts to “understated quality” as their success becomes more secure. They do not stop buying, but their reasons for buying shift, which does not happen because the world has changed, but because they have.

To develop this contextual intuition yourself, keep an eye on how major publications—and the more niche ones that your audience follows—frame current events. Not for the news itself, but for understanding how collective narratives evolve. Watch how successful brands adapt their message as contexts shift. Notice which older marketing messages suddenly feel obsolete, and why. Pay attention to subtle changes in language. During uncertainty, words like “explosive” and “massive” often trigger resistance. Terms like “stable,”


<!-- Source: Quiet Persuasion.pdf; PDF page 279 -->

“resilient,” and “sustainable” become magnetically reassuring. These shifts reveal deeper changes in collective psychology. Most importantly, develop sensitivity to the questions people ask in different contexts.

Your message exists in time as much as it exists in space. Miss the moment, and even the most powerful persuasion falls flat. Match the moment, and even simple messages resonate.

### Quiet and Loud Persuasion: The Art of Choosing Between Subtle Power and Direct Force

While loud persuasionists shout promises and guarantees, masters of quiet persuasion reshape reality itself. Understanding when to use each approach, and how to blend them, marks the difference between crude manipulation and elegant directing of energy.
